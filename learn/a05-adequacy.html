Will there be enough power when it matters? Resource adequacy and capacity accreditation

Simulate 500 years of weather and plant outages, count the days that run short, then rate what each new resource is worth

Analyst lesson A5 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: simulate 500 resampled weather years with random plant outages, estimate the loss-of-load expectation, and accredit wind, solar, a battery, demand response and peakers.

The Plains Grid over 500 simulated years (seed 2025; illustrative fleet, resampled weather)

  1. 4,000 MW of coal and gas units, 3,000 MW of wind and 1,200 MW of solar serve a 4,000 MW peak. Units fail at random.
  2. Over 500 simulated years the loss-of-load expectation is 0.228 days a year (± 0.049 from sampling alone), above the 1-in-10 criterion of 0.1. In 410 years nothing went short.
  3. Shortfalls bunch on summer evenings: 81% of the shortfall hours fall in June to September.
  4. Each 500 MW added, demand response counted as supply, lifts a nameplate reserve margin from 105.0% to 117.5%, yet LOLE ends anywhere from 0.012 to 0.200.
  5. Rated by the perfect MW that cut unserved energy as much, the five resources range from 89% (peakers) to 15% (solar) of their 500 MW (table below).
  6. Three blocks of solar in a row: 15%, then 6%, then 3%. Beside a 500 MW battery, solar's first block rates 31%.

With JavaScript on, you roll simulated years, run 500 of them with a seed you can change, and rate each resource yourself.

  • A teaching simulation: the Plains Grid, its units, their outage rates and the resources added are illustrative, set so that the grid misses the criterion before anything is added.
  • Weather is resampled, not observed: each week of a simulated year is a real week of 2024 or 2025, shifted by up to a week. The extremes are days that happened then.
  • Real shapes: demand, wind and sun of the Southwest Power Pool (EIA-930, 2024 and 2025); wind and solar scaled to EIA's 2025 monthly capacity factors, as in M3.
  • Units fail independently of each other and of the weather. Planned maintenance, the network and imports are left out.
  • A simulated year is not a forecast; LOLE, LOLH and EUE are averages over the 500 years. LOLE is shown with its Monte Carlo error, which leaves out uncertainty in the weather history and the assumptions.

What you just did, as diagrams

Resource adequacy
Enough supply in nearly every hour, judged over many weather years and plant outages.
Loss-of-load expectation (LOLE)
Shortfall days a year, averaged over the simulated years; the common criterion is 0.1, one day in ten years.
Expected unserved energy (EUE)
Unserved MWh a year: how deep and long the shortfalls are, which LOLE does not say.
Reserve-margin screen
MW installed over the peak, demand response counted as supply: quick, but blind to when the MW show up.
Accreditation
The smallest perfect MW that would cut unserved energy as much, as a share of the MW added; it changes with the mix.
Go deeper · AnalystDays or megawatt-hours: which reliability to match
  • Two measures, two answers. LOLE counts days of any shortfall; EUE weighs each by its MWh. Demand response here removes many short, shallow days but less energy, so its rating depends on which is matched.
  • This lesson matches EUE. In 500 years a count of days moves in steps of 0.002, too coarse to rate one block. PJM rates its classes by EUE too (Manual 20A, sections 2.4 and 2.6).
  • Marginal, not average. Each block is rated against the mix already present. An average can hide how the contribution of the next block differs.
Go deeper · ModelerThe simulation in five lines, and its symbols
  • Outages. Each unit is up or out, in spells of random length: out for \(r\) hours on average, up for \(r(1-f)/f\), so out a share \(f\) of the time.
  • Margin. \(M_t = \text{units}_t + 3{,}000\,w_t + 1{,}200\,s_t - 4{,}000\,d_t\) MW. A battery discharges only when \(M_t < 0\) and charges from any surplus.
  • Accreditation. The smallest \(C\) with \(\text{EUE}(\text{grid} + C) \le \text{EUE}(\text{grid} + \text{resource})\), by bisection: halve the range of MW again and again until the two match.
  • Unique, or a boundary. EUE falls strictly while above zero, so \(C\) is unique. If the resource leaves no shortfall in the sample, the smallest \(C\) is a limit of 500 years, not zero risk.
  • Correlated outages. Real studies tie outage rates to temperature (PJM’s Manual 20A, section 2.3); open tools such as PRAS do this at scale. A9 adds hazards.

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “An LOLE of 0.1 days a year means 2.4 hours without power each year.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. LOLE counts days with any shortfall, of any size or length. Here 0.228 days a year came with 0.70 shortfall hours a year, mostly short evening events.

Myth or fact? “500 MW of solar adds as much reliability as 500 MW of peakers.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In this grid the peakers rate 89% of their 500 MW and solar 15%: shortfalls come after sunset.

Myth or fact? “A resource's accreditation is a fixed property of its technology.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It depends on the grid and its mix: solar's third block rates 3% here, and its first block 31% beside a battery.

Myth or fact? “Two plans with the same reserve margin at nameplate can have very different LOLE.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. Each 500 MW added gives 117.5% at nameplate, yet LOLE ends at 0.012 with peakers and 0.200 with solar.

In 500 simulated years, 114 days had at least one hour of shortfall. What is the LOLE?

  • 114 days a year
  • 0.228 days a year
  • 0.1 days a year
  • 2.28 days a year
Answer

114 ÷ 500 = 0.228 shortfall days a year, above the 1-in-10 criterion of 0.1.

500 MW of wind cuts unserved energy as much as 79 MW of perfect capacity would. What is its accreditation?

  • 79%
  • 34%
  • 16%
  • 100%
Answer

79 ÷ 500 ≈ 16%. PJM's 34% for onshore wind is another grid's rating, from another model.
