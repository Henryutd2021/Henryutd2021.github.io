Cost and carbon done properly: one boundary, a fair baseline, a stated counterfactual

Audit a fictional bus study with four flaws, fix each one, and watch its conclusion change

Research studio R7 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: audit a fictional study of electric buses with mismatched cost and emissions boundaries, a convenient baseline, missing grid costs and a credit counted twice; fix each flaw, watch the conclusion change in your browser, and write an audit note checked against the course rubric.

The task

  1. Produce: an audit note on a study that weighs cost against carbon: each flaw, its fix and its effect, then the corrected conclusion with the condition it rests on.
  2. From: a fictional study of 40 electric buses, its tables, and your browser's recomputation of every number after each fix.
  3. Done when: the rubric's bar for Consistent accounting and for Realism: one boundary, the emissions question named, who pays named, inputs' evidence status and a list of what is left out.

About 35 minutes guided here, then about 90 minutes on your own:

  • pick a study from your field: a report, a paper or a press release;
  • run the checklist under Self-check on it;
  • recompute one number it rests on, on one boundary;
  • write the audit note and rate it against the rubric.

The study (fictional)

Electric buses for Port Alder: cheaper and carbon-free. R. Okonjo and L. Varga, Harbourline Consulting (fictional), report to Port Alder Transit (fictional), 2026.

Replacing Port Alder Transit's 40 oldest diesel buses with battery-electric buses saves $1.15 million a year and cuts the fleet's CO₂ by 100%.

Its inputs (illustrative)

Input Electric Diesel
Price per bus $1,000,000 + $60,000 charger $560,000
Energy per mile 2.2 kWh, at the depot meter, charging losses included 3.5 mpg (the 2009 buses)
Energy price $0.09 per kWh, energy rate only $3.60 per gallon
Repairs per mile $0.60 $1.20 (the 2009 buses)
Purchase credit $150,000 per bus, the state's clean-bus credit (fictional) none

Both fleets: 40 buses, 36,000 miles each a year, a 12-year life, a 3% real discount rate.

From Port Alder Transit's fleet plan (fictional): The 40 buses bought in 2009 retire in 2026; 40 new diesel buses replace them (4.5 mpg, $0.85 a mile in repairs) unless the board funds another option.

Units: t is a metric tonne (1,000 kg); mpg is miles per U.S. gallon; a demand charge is dollars per kW of the month's highest draw (a kW-month), billed each month.

Two numbers worked from these inputs
  • Charging energy: 40 buses × 36,000 miles × 2.2 kWh per mile = 3,168,000 kWh = 3,168 MWh a year.
  • Its bill: 3,168,000 kWh × $0.09 per kWh = $285,120 a year.

Two real numbers. Diesel emits 10.19 kg CO₂ per U.S. gallon burned (EIA, 2024). The U.S. grid emitted 1.53 billion tonnes over 4.18 trillion kWh generated in 2023 (EIA): 0.366 t per MWh.

Two notes on the same study, and who pays

Weak note · Developing

Audit: the study looks optimistic [1]. Electric buses still have no emissions [2], but costs are probably higher [3]. More research is needed. [4]

  1. Falls short: No flaw is named, so no one can check or fix it.
  2. Falls short: True at the tailpipe only; the power plants that charge them emit.
  3. Falls short: No number, no baseline, no 'whose cost'.
  4. Falls short: It says nothing a reader could act on.

Strong note · Proficient

Boundary: charging is in the cost but its CO₂ is not; at EIA's 2023 U.S. average factor, the electric fleet is attributed 1,160 t a year. [1] Baseline: without the project the agency buys new diesel buses (4.5 mpg), not the 2009 fleet (3.5 mpg). [2] Grid: add the depot connection ($1.80 million up front) and demand charges ($288,000 a year). [3] Credit: counted once, in the capital line; the second $500,000 a year removed. [4] Evidence: the prices, miles and 2.2 kWh per mile are the study's illustrative assumptions; the replacement plan is the agency's own document (fictional); the CO₂ factors are EIA's published figures. [5] Leaves out: making the buses and batteries, street air and noise, grid losses, a grid that gets cleaner, battery replacement. [6] Conclusion: for the agency, electric costs $0.65 million a year more and lowers attributed CO₂ by 2,101 t (64%), about $309 per tonne, if the grid is at the 2023 U.S. average. [7]

  2. Works: The counterfactual, stated from the agency's own plan.
  3. Works: Up front and annual kept apart.
  4. Works: Says which count stays and why.
  5. Works: Each key input's evidence status, as the Realism bar asks.
  6. Works: What the audit does not count, said.

Where the audited conclusion holds

It holds when It leaves out
  • the inputs are this fictional study's, in 2025 dollars, over a 12-year life at 3%;
  • costs are the agency's, with the state's credit once; CO₂ is each fleet's operating energy, at the tailpipe or the power plant;
  • charging's CO₂ is attributed at the grid's average factor (EIA, 2023): what the grid really emits less needs a consequential analysis (A10);
  • 2.2 kWh per mile is at the depot meter, charging losses included;
  • the agency pays the depot connection; both fleets run the same routes and miles.
  • making the buses and batteries: embodied CO₂ is outside both fleets' counts (a life-cycle boundary, A11);
  • grid losses between plant and depot (EIA: about 5%), which would raise charging's CO₂ a little;
  • tailpipe air pollution and noise in the streets, which electric buses cut;
  • a grid that gets cleaner over the 12 years, and battery replacement;
  • federal grants, fuel and power price changes.

How far up the ladder is each number?

  • The transit board voted the money for 40 electric buses. Funded. Funded: money committed, nothing built yet.
  • Every permit the depot's new service connection needs has been issued. Permitted. Permitted: the authorities have said yes, but nothing is built yet; its cost estimate is still an estimate.
  • Ten electric buses have carried passengers since March. Operating. Operating: in service, but a few months do not show a year's energy use.
  • For a full year, winter included, the ten buses ran their planned routes within their energy target. At design output. At design output: planned service at a measured 2.2 kWh per mile.
  • The audit's cost per attributed tonne of CO₂. Not on the ladder: a projection. A projection: a model's output from inputs on several rungs, not a stage of anything built.

  • The audit counts charging at the grid's average factor: the buses' share of what the plants emit.
  • What charging changes depends on which plants respond in the hours the buses charge, now and as the grid is built out: A10's questions 2 and 3.

Cost and carbon, before you trust a result

  • Same service: Both options deliver the same service: the same routes and miles, the same years.
  • One boundary: Cost and CO₂ cover the same service, alternatives and years; whose costs count and which emissions stages are in are stated, with what is left out.
  • A stated counterfactual: One sentence says what happens without the project, from a plan or a rule, not a convenient past.
  • The emissions question: Average or marginal factor, and why: whose share of the grid's CO₂, or what a change in demand changes.
  • Grid and connection costs: Upgrades, demand charges and connection fees are priced, with who pays them.
  • Credits once: Each grant or credit appears in one line, and is a transfer for the state and the agency together.
  • Up front and annual apart: One-time amounts and their annual equivalents are labelled, with the rate and the life.
  • The condition that moves it: The input that changes the conclusion most is named, with the result at its other values.

  • Fact

Myth. It moves money from the state to the agency: the audited buses cost the agency $0.65 million a year more, and the two together $1.25 million.

Myth or fact? “The fair baseline is the fleet running today.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It is what would happen without the project: here the replacement plan's new diesel buses, not the 2009 ones.

In the real world

In the reported scenarios, reductions in attributed emissions were on the order of one to two percent of regional totals or less.

Reductions are computed under average-emission-intensity accounting with perfect foresight; they are not estimates of the consequential (marginal) system response.

From: Li et al. (2025), Cell Reports Sustainability.

Reading it. Attributed emissions: a user's share of the grid's CO₂, here at its average intensity, as in this studio's audit.

Perfect foresight: the model knows the coming hours in advance. Consequential response: how much the grid's own emissions actually change.
