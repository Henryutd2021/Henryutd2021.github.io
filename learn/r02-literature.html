How do you find out what is already known?

Search, snowball and map twelve papers

The task

  1. Produce: a search log, a concept matrix of 12 papers with an empty cell searched directly, a second-pass extraction sheet on the closest paper, and a gap statement with its evidence.
  2. From: the question below, two open databases (OpenAlex and one more, such as Semantic Scholar or Crossref) and one review to snowball from.
  3. Done when: the rubric's bar for positioning: a concept matrix of at least 12 sources and a stated gap with evidence.

About 30 minutes guided here, then about 4 hours on your own.

The worked question: How do capacity-expansion models stand in for a whole year with a few representative periods, and what happens to storage and to extreme periods when they do?

Reading the closest paper: Hilbers, Brayshaw and Gandy (2023)

  1. First pass (5–10 min): Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusions; glance at the references you already know. Out: The five Cs: category, context, correctness, contributions, clarity.
  2. Second pass (up to an hour): Figures, tables and the argument; skip the proofs. Mark the references to read next. Out: The extraction sheet, filled.
  3. Third pass, optional (4–5 hours for a beginner, outside this studio's 4 hours): Rebuild the work under the same assumptions, and challenge each one. Out: What you would do differently, and what it would change.

Its headings (open preprint, October 2022), for the first pass: 1. Introduction (capacity expansion planning; time series aggregation; inter-period links and storage) · 2. This paper's contribution · 3. Methods (overview; framework; remarks) · 4. Simulation studies (overview; setup; validation; example; discussion) · 5. Conclusions (conclusions; extensions).

The first pass, worked: the five Cs
Category
What kind of paper is it? A methods paper: a new aggregation framework, tested in simulations on a six-region test system.
Context
Which papers does it build on? Two from your matrix: Kotzur et al. (2018) for linked storage, and Teichgraeber, Küpper and Brandt (2021) for extreme periods taken from model results.
Correctness
Which assumptions will you check in pass two? A test system based on the IEEE six-bus system; demand and wind only, no solar; operation with perfect foresight.
Contributions
What is new? Periods chosen after a first model run that keep chronology for storage and keep the extreme periods that drive capacity; models, data and code released.
Clarity
Is it well written? Your judgement: note one thing you would ask the authors.
The second pass, worked: the extraction sheet
Question
Can choosing periods after a first model run cut the error of capacity plans built from long weather series, in models with storage?
Scale and boundary
A six-region test grid: baseload, peaking and wind generation, transmission and storage, built from scratch.
Data vintage
Hourly demand and wind for European countries, 1980–2017, from a published reanalysis-based dataset.
Method
Capacity-expansion optimisation on representative days, then an operations run over the full series.
Counterfactual
Three conventional schemes chosen from the input data alone and, on 3-year samples, the plan from the full series.
Sensitivity and validation
30 and 120 representative days; 40 resampled series; unserved energy checked over the full series.
Not modelled
Solar; operation without perfect foresight (named by the authors as an extension).
Code and data
Public, with a Zenodo DOI.

Write the gap statement

Draft your gap statement: what you searched (where, when, strings), what the matrix shows, what the direct search found, what a second pass on the closest paper showed, and the question that remains.

Your own literature map: about 4 hours

  1. Write two concept blocks for your own question; run five logged strings in OpenAlex and one other open database, such as Semantic Scholar or Crossref (string, date, count). (30 min)
  2. Screen titles and abstracts down to about 30 candidates; note why you dropped the rest. (30 min)
  3. Find one review; snowball backward and forward from it; add what the searches missed. (30 min)
  4. Do a first pass on 12 papers (about 7 minutes each) and code them in a concept matrix with a written coding rule. (90 min)
  5. Search your empty cell directly and log the string, date and count. (15 min)
  6. Read the closest paper in a second pass, fill the extraction sheet, then write the gap statement. (45 min)

Optional, beyond the 4 hours: a third pass on the closest paper (4–5 hours for a beginner).

With JavaScript on, you build the strings yourself, predict each count, code four papers and test the empty cell step by step; your drafts stay in this browser.

Two answers to the same task

Weak answer · Developing

Representative periods are widely used, but few studies [1] consider storage and extreme events together [2]. This study is the first [3] to fill this gap [4].

  1. Falls short: No count, no database, no date: a reader cannot check it.
  2. Falls short: Undefined: linked between periods? Extremes chosen how?
  3. Falls short: A novelty claim the evidence cannot carry; a direct search finds at least three papers that do both.
  4. Falls short: Asserted, not shown: no matrix, no search of the cell.

Strong answer · Proficient

Search: OpenAlex titles and abstracts, 3 November 2026, five logged strings [1]; one review snowballed both ways [2]. Matrix: 12 papers coded from their abstracts by a written rule [3]; no abstract reports both a storage link and extreme periods [4]. Direct check: a search of that cell returned 23 works; by their abstracts, at least three do both [5] (Hilbers et al. 2023; Moradi-Sepahvand and Tindemans 2023, 2024). Closest work: a second pass on Hilbers et al. finds a six-region test grid with demand and wind only, no solar [6]. Remaining question: does choosing periods after a first model run still keep extremes and storage right once solar is added? The two other papers get a second pass before we claim it [7].

  1. Works: Database, date and strings: anyone can repeat the search and see what has changed since.
  2. Works: Search and snowballing find different papers; together they found all twelve.
  3. Works: The rubric's bar for positioning: at least 12 sources, coded with a stated rule.
  4. Works: Said as the coding allows: what the abstracts report, not what the papers lack.
  5. Works: The empty cell is tested, not assumed, and the gap as first stated does not survive.
  6. Works: The closest work is read, not just named: the extraction sheet supplies the distinction.

Go deeperWhy the course never calls a question “novel”

Few papers on a topic is not evidence that a question is new, important or feasible. It can mean:

  • the field uses other words;
  • the field is small;
  • the question was tried and dropped.

The rubric asks for a gap stated with evidence; its Strong level asks that the gap survive a reviewer's search.

The question studio (R10) shows how much has been written on a topic only as context, for the same reason.

Go deeperThe same workflow for other question types

The twelve papers here share one method family, optimization models. That is this example's choice, not the studio's: optimization is one family among several (R3).

Search, snowballing and the matrix work for every question type (R1). Only the matrix columns change, to fit the evidence each type uses. The questions below are illustrative.

Type Question Matrix columns
Descriptive How often did a region's wind output stay under a tenth of its capacity for a whole day? Data source and years · time resolution · how a low day is defined
Explanatory Why did members of some rural co-ops adopt rooftop solar faster than others? Data (survey, records, interviews) · method (statistics, case study) · setting
Evaluative Did a time-of-use tariff lower household peak demand, compared with a flat rate? Comparison group · design (trial, difference-in-differences, matching) · metered or surveyed
Predictive What will a city's summer peak demand be in ten years, and with what uncertainty? Forecast method · how uncertainty is shown · validation period
Qualitative (awareness level) How do households describe their winter heating choices? Who was asked, and how many · interviews or documents · how answers were coded
Go deeperWhat this studio leaves out
  • Systematic reviews with a registered protocol, two independent screeners and a flow diagram of every record: the next step when a review is itself the study.
  • Reference managers: any one works if it stores the DOI, your tags and the date you added each paper.
  • Grey literature (agency reports, grid-operator studies): search the agencies' own sites and log them the same way.
