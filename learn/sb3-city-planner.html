City Planner

Plan Prairie City’s heat, cars and rooftop solar, run its energy hub hour by hour against each district’s substation limit, and see what changes for each household group

Sandbox SB3 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: plan a fictional city’s heat pumps, retrofits, district heat, electric cars, rooftop solar and batteries, run its energy hub as a linear program against each district’s substation limit, and read a screening estimate of who pays.

The brief

The city
Prairie City (fictional): 250,000 people in a dense core, suburbs and a rural fringe, each behind one substation (a teaching simplification); 2025's homes, prices and eight representative days.
You decide
How many furnaces become heat pumps; retrofits; a district-heat network in the core; electric cars (EVs) and how they charge; rooftop solar; batteries; whether substations may be upgraded.
The engine works out
A linear program (LP) runs the city's energy hub hour by hour at the least cost, each hour within each substation's limit, and buys upgrades only where the busiest hour needs them.
Who pays
Bills by household group and their energy burden: home energy bills as a share of income. Groups are bands of area median income (AMI).
It leaves out
Power flow inside a district, outages, growth to 2040, the cars' prices, and how bills are really set: the household table is a screening estimate, not an output of the LP.
It assumes
Every heat pump is large enough for the coldest modeled hour: equipment sizes and their limits are not modeled.

Open-ended: start from a preset, change one thing at a time, and copy the plan link to keep a plan. Prairie City is fictional; its numbers are illustrative.

Siting with map layers is in the capstone C3: the same engine, with A19's map for a solar farm.

  • Illustrative: Prairie City, its districts, substation limits and household groups (prairie-city.json); retrofit, district-heat, store and upgrade costs are set for teaching, within ranges you can change.
  • Assumed: the heat network's gas boiler already exists (fuel only); managed cars are plugged in whenever needed; the weather is NOAA's normals on the Southwest Power Pool's (SPP's) 2025 days (synthetic).
  • Based on public data: Kansas's 2025 prices (EIA), Wichita's weather normals (NOAA), SPP's 2025 hourly shapes (EIA-930), eGRID's 2023 SPP North CO₂ rate (EPA), DOE LEAD's income bands.
  • Costs: 2025 dollars; heat pumps and rooftop solar from EIA (the heat pump's 15-year life set for teaching), batteries from NREL, paid yearly at 4% a year after inflation (a real rate) over each item's life.

How the engine works, as diagrams

The hub
Each hour, electricity through the substation, gas and rooftop sun must meet each district's heat, base load and car charging; heat pumps, furnaces, a heat network, batteries and managed charging sit between.
The limit
Each substation's flow stays below its limit every hour, unless the plan buys more room; the busiest hour decides, not the year's total.
It holds when
Eight representative days stand for 2025, storage shifts energy only within a day, and 2025's prices, people and homes stay as they are.
The full-year check
The presets, run again on every hour of 2025: total yearly cost and CO₂ within 0.1% (the change against 2025 moves by a little more).
What it tests
The year is scaled, like the days, to the same annual heat and base energy, so the totals agree partly by construction: it checks the hours' timing.
Cold days in the year
The tightest hours fall on 19 or 20 February, two other days the design day stands for; plans that buy upgrades need a little more room.
Who pays
Bills and energy burden by household group come from an accounting layer after the LP, with a stated rule for who adopts first.
Go deeper · ModelerThe linear program behind the bench
  • The formulation: M12 writes the hub down, with building shares as decisions, the balance in each district-hour and the substation’s ceiling.
  • Its duals: the ceiling’s dual is the value of more room; a CO₂ cap’s dual is the cost of the last tonne.
  • The notebook: M12’s rebuilds the model in Python with SciPy and HiGHS.
  • The capstone: C3 plans the city to 2040 on this engine.
Go deeperWhen two answers cost the same: the tie rule

With one electricity price all day, many schedules cost the same: a car can charge at 01:00 or 03:00. Small stated costs pick one; they are left out of every reported cost total, but the LP's duals include them:

  • $20 a year per MW of each district's busiest hour, and $10 per MW of the three districts' busiest hour together: flexible loads spread out.
  • $1 per MWh out of a battery and $0.50 per MWh out of the heat store: no cycling for nothing.
  • $0.10 per MWh of managed charging, times the hour's share of SPP's peak demand: quieter hours first.

So the LP minimizes the cost plus these small costs. On a few plans it pays a little to keep a busiest hour that is under its limit lower:

  • The price of the ties: under $30 a year in the engine's test, against the cheapest schedule found without them.
  • The same answer: every preset run again with the costs ×2 and ×0.1 gives the same numbers.
  • One answer: each busiest hour, upgrade and the energy bought stays within the test's tolerances (0.5 MW, 0.05 MW and 5 MWh) over all plans within $1 of the best cost.
  • The duals: where a limit binds without an upgrade, the reported cost saves about $9 per MW a year more than the dual shows: the tie costs' share.
  • Your plans: not checked this way; their hourly schedules can still differ.
