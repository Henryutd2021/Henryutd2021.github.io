Keep the campus running: a microgrid for a three-day winter outage

Plan the microgrid that keeps power to the hospital, its water and heating plant and the dorms through a winter storm, compare three owners, and hand in a one-page note

Capstone C1 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: size Prairie Campus’s solar, battery and generator sets on the Microgrid Workbench’s engine so the county hospital, its water and heating plant and the dorms ride through a three-day winter storm within the feeder’s limits; compare utility, co-op and community ownership; hand in a plan link and a one-page note.

The brief

Prairie Campus's board must choose a microgrid that keeps electricity flowing to the county hospital, its water and heating plant and the dorms through a three-day winter storm, within the host feeder's limits.

Assume that powering these loads keeps their heating equipment working: heat demand and indoor temperature are not modeled.

  1. Hand in: a plan link and a one-page note with the annual cost, the unserved critical load, the emissions, a comparison of three owners and a sensitivity line (Modeler version: the M5 notebook too).
  2. From: the Microgrid Workbench's engine and a hypothetical outage from 20 to 22 January 2025 (a teaching solar profile, a fictional campus).
  3. Done when: every check under the note is in place, the brief's three loads served among them, and the self-check reaches Proficient on every competence.

About 90 minutes: 35 to build and run the plan, 35 to write the note, 20 to check it.

In the real world

In an industry internship, the course author worked on this question: how should solar PV, battery storage and backup generation be sized for an industrial campus to meet demand through grid outages?

The work used a techno-economic optimization model to size a resilient microgrid for the campus.

The company's data, models and results are confidential and are not shown here.

From the course author's research: in the modeled supply chains, each open factory or warehouse could run its own microgrid, with on-site wind, solar and storage, an energy balance every hour, and energy bought from and sold to the grid.

Holds when: stylized networks with synthetic distances and costs, candidate sites in California, Arizona and Nevada, and one year, planned week by week for products and hour by hour for energy. Leaves out: grid outages, which the studies do not model.

Approved excerpt of: Li et al. (2023), Applied Mathematical Modelling.

Write your one-page note

  1. Title and plan link: the plan's link from the bench, and the date
  2. Recommendation: Two or three sentences: what to build, what stays on, and why.
  3. The plan: each choice, with its unit
  4. Results: each result, with its unit and boundary
  5. Assumptions: one sentence each
  6. Sensitivity: how one result moves when one input changes, and whether the plan was held fixed
  7. Owner, and who pays: One or two sentences: which owner, who pays, who decides.
  8. What the plan cannot do: One or two sentences: what could still leave the hospital short.
  9. Limits of this analysis: what the model leaves out

Check your note

Weak note · Developing

Build a big battery and lots of solar [1] so the hospital stays on. It will pay for itself [2], and the campus will be resilient [3].

  1. Falls short: No sizes and no units: the board cannot price or check it.
  2. Falls short: No number, no boundary, no dollar year: pays against what, over what time?
  3. Falls short: Resilient against which outage? Say the scenario and what the plan cannot survive.

Strong note · Proficient

Recommendation: build 3,225 kW of solar, a 5,050 kWh battery and 2 generator sets with 675 gallons of diesel [1]. It keeps the hospital, the water and heating plant and the dorms powered for all 72 hours of the storm (0 kWh short). Cost: $1,181,114 a year, community-owned, 2025 dollars: equipment, upkeep and the normal-year grid bill [2], $224,472 below the grid-only bill. The storm's 675 gallons of diesel ($2,470) are extra [3]. Sensitivity: 0 kWh short for a storm of 1 to 3 days, but 4,508 kWh on a fourth day, the plan held fixed [4]. Emissions: 6.9 t of CO₂ from the storm's diesel; 1,645 t from a normal year's grid electricity (U.S. average, operating only) [5]. Assumptions: a full battery and tank when the storm starts, the coming sun known, flat loads [6]. Owner: community. A co-op would pay $1,124,657 a year for the same plan, a utility $1,240,076; community owners decide by their own rules and share the cost, worth $56,457 a year more than a co-op here [7]. Limits: one storm tested; a set that fails to start, part-load fuel use and fuel deliveries are left out [8].

  1. Works: Sizes with units, and the fuel that limits the sets.
  2. Works: The cost with its boundary and dollar year (illustrative costs).
  3. Works: Storm fuel stated on its own: adding it to an annual cost would need an outage frequency.
  4. Works: The sensitivity, with the plan held fixed: the plan's limit, said plainly.
  5. Works: Emissions with their boundary: operating CO₂, average factor.
  6. Works: The assumptions that flatter the plan, named.
  7. Works: Who pays and who decides, and why this owner over a cheaper one: a reasoned choice, not the cheapest by default.
  8. Works: What the analysis leaves out, so the board does not over-read it.
