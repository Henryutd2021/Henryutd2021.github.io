Analyst checkpoint: an auditable spreadsheet for a new case

About two and a half hours: build a spreadsheet for a school’s rooftop solar and battery, say who pays and who benefits, test the result, then check it against a worked workbook

The Analyst checkpoint (CK-A) of Energy Systems, Step by Step: build an auditable spreadsheet for a fictional school’s rooftop solar and battery from dated EIA and NLR numbers: a boundary, an assumptions log with sources, the school’s net present value, who pays and who benefits (transfers against real costs), and a sensitivity of the main result. A worked workbook with live formulas opens when you submit. Answers and times stay in your browser.

Task

How this checkpoint works · about 150 minutes, no hints

  1. A · Boundary and log (about 40 min). Download the starter workbook. State your boundary; finish the assumptions log: every input's type, source, date and, where you will test it, a low and a high value.
  2. B · The model (about 50 min). Convert the costs to 2025 dollars and build the school's yearly saving and its net present value (NPV), every number a formula.
  3. C · Who pays (about 20 min). Split the school's bill saving into a real cost saved and a transfer; add the parties up (refresher: A13).
  4. D · Sensitivity (about 20 min). Swing the inputs that matter one at a time and report the main result as a range.
  5. Submit (about 20 min). Enter your results in the nine tasks below, in any order: the worked workbook opens once all nine are answered.

How it is marked, before you start

  • Proficient: at least 5 of the 7 auto-marked tasks right on the first try, plus at least 3 of the 4 points of each written task (1 and 9), which you tick yourself.
  • Strong: at least 6 of 7 on the first try and every written point.
  • Opening solutions early: a task whose answer you open before answering counts as not right (a written task: no points). Opening the worked workbook before all nine are answered makes the attempt practice, with no level.
  • Written tasks: your text stays as you first submit it, and that is the text you rate.
  • After the worked workbook opens: an answer given after that counts as practice, not as right, and a written task then earns no points.

Starter workbook (.xlsx)

It opens in Excel, LibreOffice Calc, Google Sheets or Apple Numbers. It holds the case's facts with their sources and an empty layout: no formulas, no answers.

Refreshers: A1 (cash flows), A4 (tariffs), A13 (who pays), K3 (discounting), K7 (spreadsheets).

The case

A school's rooftop solar and battery

Situation: Prairie Town High School's district is offered a rooftop solar array and a battery behind the school's meter.

Deliverable: An auditable spreadsheet that tells the district what it would gain or lose, who else pays or benefits, and how sure that answer is.

Inputs, illustrative where marked

  • System: 250 kW-DC of rooftop solar and a 150 kW / 300 kWh battery, installed together
  • School: uses 1,000,000 kWh a year, with a billed peak of 400 kW every month (illustrative)
  • Tariff: a demand charge of $10 per kW-month plus an energy charge, set so the school's bill averages Kansas's 2025 commercial price, 11.17 cents/kWh (EIA); exports earn 3 cents/kWh (illustrative)
  • Battery power: cuts the monthly billed peak by 60% of its power on average (illustrative)
  • Battery energy: about 200 full cycles a year, each taking in 300 kWh from the school's supply and returning 90%; the 10% lost is bought at the energy charge (illustrative)
  • Costs: from EIA's 2024 distributed-generation report (2022 dollars) and NLR's 2025 ATB (2023 dollars); convert to 2025 dollars
  • Money: 25 years, real 2025 dollars, a 4% real discount rate (the course's), battery replaced at the end of year 15
  • End value: at year 25 the replacement battery has 5 of its 15 years left: count 5/15 of its cost as its residual value (straight line)
  • The utility: avoids 3 cents for each kWh it no longer supplies and, for half of the battery's peak cut, $180 per kW a year of capacity (illustrative; lesson A4's unit cost)
  • Rate reset: assume the utility recovers its whole remaining revenue shortfall from its other customers at the next rate reset

Prairie Town and its high school are fictional; the school's load and several inputs are illustrative.

Without JavaScript nothing here is locked: the worked solution is further down the page. Build your spreadsheet first, write your answers down, then compare.

  1. Your boundary Part A · State the boundary

    In two or three sentences: whose money does your main result count, over what period and in which dollars, and what does it leave out?

    Self-rated against 4 points, shown after you submit.

  2. Solar output Part B · A number with units

    How much electricity does the array make in a year? Within 2% counts as right.

    Answer with a number and a unit.

  3. Up-front cost in 2025 dollars Part B · Convert dollar years

    What does the system cost up front, in 2025 dollars? Within 1% counts as right.

    Answer with a number and a unit.

  4. The school's bill saving Part B · Read a tariff

    How much lower is the school's electricity bill in a year (gross: before upkeep)? Within 3% counts as right.

    Answer with a number and a unit.

  5. The main result Part B · Present value

    Using the brief's assumptions, including its residual value, by how much does the present value of the school's costs exceed that of its savings? Within 3% counts as right.

    Answer with a number and a unit.

  6. Who pays the gap Part C · Transfers against real costs

    Part of the school's bill saving is not a saving in the utility's costs. Under the brief's rate-reset assumption, who pays it?

    • a. The school district
    • b. The utility's other customers, at the next rate reset
    • c. Federal taxpayers
    • d. Nobody: every dollar the school saves is a real cost saved

  7. The cost shift Part C · A screening estimate

    Under the same assumption, how much a year moves to the utility's other customers? Within 5% counts as right.

    Answer with a number and a unit.

  8. What moves the result most Part D · Sensitivity

    Swinging one input at a time over the ranges in the worked log, which moves the school's NPV most?

    • a. The up-front cost, ±25%
    • b. The discount rate, 2% to 7%
    • c. The demand charge, $5 to $20 per kW-month, with the energy charge re-set so the pre-project bill is unchanged
    • d. The average electricity price, ±20%

  9. Your log and your sensitivity Part D · Audit trail

    Paste three rows of your assumptions log and your sensitivity statement (the main result as a range, and the input that moves it most).

    Self-rated against 4 points, shown after you submit.

Prairie Town is fictional; its numbers are illustrative where the log says so. Prices, costs, the capacity factor and the price index are dated public figures (Sources).

Nothing you type leaves this browser unless you copy it. The copied summary includes what you typed in tasks 1 and 9.

Worked solution

Opens once all nine tasks are answered. The checkpoint asks for your own spreadsheet first, so the worked workbook waits for it. Opening it now makes this attempt practice, with no level.

The answer in one line

Net present value to the school: −$776,324 (2025 dollars, 25 years, 4% real). In present value the district pays $776,000 more than it saves.

One-at-a-time swings put it between −$993,000 and −$560,000: no single swing changes its sign. Cumulative cash stays below zero in every year (lowest −$1,020,000), so there is no payback within 25 years.

The points of the two written tasks

Your boundary

  • Whose money: the school district's, from its electricity bill, the system's capital, upkeep and the battery's replacement.
  • Time and money: 25 years, real 2025 dollars, one real discount rate (4%), with costs from other dollar years converted.
  • Left out, and said so: tax credits, financing, resilience, CO2's value and solar wear (or a stated subset of these).
  • The other parties (the utility's other customers, federal taxpayers, the resource view) reported apart from the main result.

Your log and your sensitivity

  • Every input has a value, a unit, a type (sourced, course data, illustrative, choice, derived) and a source or a reason.
  • Sourced values name the document, the table or page, and an as-of date.
  • Costs from different dollar years are converted to 2025 dollars with a stated index.
  • The main result is given as a range from the sensitivity, with the input that moves it most named.

Rubric

The checkpoint's level

  • Proficient: at least 5 of the 7 auto-marked tasks right on the first try; at least 3 of the 4 points of your boundary (task 1) and 3 of the 4 points of your log and sensitivity (task 9).
  • Strong: at least 6 of 7 on the first try, and every point of both written tasks.
  • Developing: at least 3 of 7 on the first try.
  • Not yet: anything else. The checkpoint shows readiness for the Modeler tier; it certifies nothing.
  • Opened early: a task whose answer you open before answering counts as not right; if the worked workbook opens before all nine tasks are answered (on purpose, or in an earlier attempt), the attempt is practice and gets no level.
  • Answered late: an answer given after the worked workbook opened counts as practice, not as right; a written task submitted after it earns no points.

The written tasks are self-rated against their points. A teacher or mentor can rate your workbook again with the course rubric below, from the copied summary and the file.
