What you just did, as diagrams

A microgrid
Loads and sources inside one electrical boundary, run as one unit by a controller. A grid-connected one can open its switch and run alone; an isolated one, like the village, always does.
Forming an island
A grid-forming source, such as a battery's inverter or a generator, sets voltage and frequency; ordinary solar inverters follow it.
Power, then energy
Power (kW) decides which loads the island can carry at once; stored energy and fuel (kWh) decide for how long.
Seasons
The same campus kept 650 kW for 20 full hours in July and 8 in January: weaker sun drains the battery sooner, and the 400 kW generator alone cannot carry the load, though fuel remains.
No grid at all
A village mini-grid runs as an island every day; running big loads while the sun shines keeps its battery small.

The parts of a microgrid

  1. Grid feeder. The utility's distribution line that brings grid power to the campus.
  2. Switch to the grid. The point of common coupling: the one place the campus meets the grid. Opening it islands the campus.
  3. Controller. Decides when to island, which sources run and which loads stay on.
  4. Solar panels, 600 kW. Their inverters are grid-following: they follow a voltage that something else sets, so they cannot start an island alone.
  5. Battery, 500 kW and 2,000 kWh. Its grid-forming inverter is designed to start the island and set its voltage and frequency.
  6. Diesel generator, 400 kW. Fuel for 24 hours at full power. Once started, it can form an island too.
  7. County hospital, 300 kW. Critical load: it must stay on.
  8. Water and heating plant, 100 kW. Critical too: the hospital needs water and heat.
  9. Dorms, 250 kW. Can go dark in an emergency, if they must.
  10. Classrooms and labs, 550 kW. The largest load, and the first to shed.

Go deeper · AnalystDoes the microgrid pay off?
  • Keeping the same loads on for longer may need more storage or fuel: a bigger battery, a bigger tank.
  • Whether that pays depends on what an hour without power costs the hospital, a farm or a household. Analyst lesson A14 prices an outage and sizes the campus against it.
  • Who owns the microgrid decides who pays for it and who carries the risk: a utility, a co-op, the county or a private company.

Check yourself

Practice, about 10 minutes: three myths, two questions, then sort six systems.

Myth or fact? “My rooftop solar keeps my lights on in a blackout.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth, for an ordinary grid-connected system: its inverter stops feeding an unintentional island, normally within 2 seconds (IEEE 1547-2018). Backup needs an isolating switch and equipment, such as a battery, that can form an island.

Myth or fact? “A microgrid is just a big backup generator.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. A microgrid runs local loads and sources as one unit under a controller, and a grid-connected one can island. A generator alone, with no controller or local network, is backup.

Myth or fact? “Doubling the battery doubles how long the hospital stays lit.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Keeping the hospital and the water plant from midnight on 28 July 2025, 2,000 kWh gives 51 full hours and 4,000 kWh gives 67: the fuel and the sun set the rest.

A battery holds 2,000 kWh and can give 500 kW. The hospital and the water plant draw 400 kW, with no sun and no generator. How long does the battery carry them?

  • 4 hours
  • 5 hours
  • 8 hours
Answer

5 hours: 2,000 kWh ÷ 400 kW. The 400 kW fits under the battery's 500 kW, so its energy is the limit.

Same battery, still no sun or generator, but the labs stay on too: 950 kW. What stops the island?

  • Power: 950 kW is more than the battery's 500 kW
  • Energy: 2,000 kWh runs out
Answer

Power. The battery cannot give more than 500 kW at once, so the island falls short at once, long before its 2,000 kWh run out.

Microgrid or not? Sort six systems

  • A campus with solar, a battery, a generator, a controller and one switch to the grid
  • A village's panels, batteries and wires, with no grid connection at all
  • A house with rooftop solar and an ordinary grid-connected inverter
  • A standby generator on its own, with no controller, that starts after the grid fails
  • A wind farm connected to the transmission grid
  • An island's diesel generators, wind turbines and batteries, with no link to a mainland
Answers
  • A microgrid: a campus with solar, a battery, a generator, a controller and one switch to the grid. Loads and sources inside one boundary, run as one unit, and able to island.
  • A microgrid: a village's panels, batteries and wires, with no grid connection at all. An isolated microgrid: it runs as an island all the time.
  • Not a microgrid: a house with rooftop solar and an ordinary grid-connected inverter. It cannot run alone: its inverter stops when the grid does.
  • Not a microgrid: a standby generator on its own, with no controller, that starts after the grid fails. Backup only: nothing runs sources and loads as one unit. A microgrid can still serve one building.
  • Not a microgrid: a wind farm connected to the transmission grid. A power plant that sells to the grid; it serves no local loads of its own.
  • A microgrid: an island's diesel generators, wind turbines and batteries, with no link to a mainland. A whole small grid run as one unit, isolated from any larger one.

In the real world

In Puerto Rico, a small microgrid that serves a community organization kept the power on for nine days during Hurricane Fiona, while other parts of the island were dark (U.S. DOE, 8 November 2023).

In an industry internship, the course author worked on this question: how should solar PV, battery storage and backup generation be sized for an industrial campus to meet demand through grid outages?

The work used a techno-economic optimization model to size a resilient microgrid for the campus.
