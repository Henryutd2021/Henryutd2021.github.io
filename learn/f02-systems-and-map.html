How does everything connect? Stocks, flows, feedback and the whole-system map

Fill a reservoir, close a feedback loop, then pull a lever and watch the whole system ripple

F2 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: run a stock-and-flow simulator (a reservoir, a battery, a carbon budget), sign and name feedback loops (chargers and EVs, the rebound effect), then pull four levers on a whole-system map and follow the ripples across technology, cost, emissions, people and security.

The lab's three stations, in brief

A reservoir
Holding up to 350 GWh and fed by a river averaging 101 MW, it gets through the year without spilling or running dry only with a steady release from 100 to 115 MW (the slider moves in 5 MW steps).
A battery
A 25 MW, 100 MWh battery that loses 20% on charging must buy 125 MWh to deliver 100.
A carbon budget
If an illustrative nation halves its 100 Mt a year by 2035 and holds there, its cumulative emissions still rise, from 825 Mt to 2,575 Mt by 2070, past a 1,500 Mt budget.
Feedback loops
More EVs bring more chargers, which bring more EVs: a reinforcing loop. Longer queues at chargers slow the growth, and the rebound effect takes back part of an efficiency saving: balancing loops.
The system map
Each of four levers reaches technology, cost, emissions, people and security within three waves.
Lever First wave Later waves
Gas price doubles Wholesale price and the gas import bill rise; the plant mix shifts; CO₂ depends on what replaces gas Gas heating bills and the energy burden rise; investment shifts and dependence on imported gas falls
A heat wave Demand rises; lines carry less; spare capacity falls; heat-related health risk rises Prices and CO₂ can rise; bills and burden can rise later; tight hours can encourage new capacity
A carbon price Gas plants can move ahead of coal plants; wholesale price rises; if gas replaces coal, CO₂ and local air pollution fall Bills rise if the tariff passes the price on; the burden depends on the revenue; jobs shift
A new data center Demand rises; it needs new connections; spare capacity falls when it connects; jobs and tax base can rise; cooling water depends on the design From its first hours, CO₂ depends on which plants serve it; later, who pays for upgrades depends on the tariff, and new supply may follow

With JavaScript on, you run these yourself: three tanks, two loops and four levers.

Tanks: the reservoir, lesson 9's battery and the nation's budget are illustrative.
Units: Mt = million tonnes; GWh = a thousand MWh.
Map: first-round directions of change, other things equal, for a grid like the Plains Grid; it states no sizes.
Waves: steps in tracing causes; each label says roughly when a step plays out.
Data center: an illustrative 300 MW load.

What you just did, as diagrams

Stock
A stock is an amount that builds up or drains: water, stored energy, cumulative CO₂, cars on the road.
Flow
A flow is a rate that fills or drains a stock; only flows change a stock.
Reinforcing loop
An even number of − links round a feedback loop: change feeds itself (reinforcing).
Balancing loop
An odd number of − links: the loop opposes the change that started it (balancing); with long delays it can overshoot.
Ripple
One change spreads step by step across technology, cost, emissions, people and security: some effects at once, some years later.
Go deeper · AnalystThe stocks that shape energy systems

Many slow, hard questions are questions about stocks.

  • Equipment. Power plants, buildings, cars and boilers last for decades, so today’s choices stay in the stock long after: new purchases are the inflow, retirements the outflow. National pathways track this (Analyst lesson A20; Modeler lesson M13).
  • Fuel. Gas in storage and emergency oil stocks buffer a supply shock (Analyst lesson A21).
  • Stored energy. Reservoirs and batteries move energy in time (lesson 9; Analyst lesson A8).
  • Cumulative CO₂ emissions. Warming tracks them: the IPCC’s best estimate is 0.45 °C for each 1,000 Gt of CO₂ (Gt = billion tonnes; likely 0.27 to 0.63 °C).
  • Net zero. Stabilizing human-induced warming requires net zero CO₂. The lab’s counter records emissions only; land and oceans absorb part, so the CO₂ left in the air is a different stock.
Go deeper · ModelerFrom diagrams to equations

A stock is a state variable that carries over from one step to the next:

\[ S_{t+1} = S_t + \big(\mathit{in}_t - \mathit{out}_t\big)\,\Delta t \]

with \(S_t\) the stock, \(\mathit{in}_t\) and \(\mathit{out}_t\) the flows and \(\Delta t\) the step. A reservoir or a battery adds physical limits, \(0 \le S_t \le \bar{S}\) with \(\bar{S}\) its capacity; a carbon budget is a chosen limit that emissions can exceed. Flow × time must come out in the stock’s unit:

  • Reservoir: change in GWh = (river in − release, in MW) × 168 hours ÷ 1,000, since 1 GWh = 1,000 MWh.
  • Carbon: change in Mt = emissions in Mt a year × 1 year.
  • Battery: the rule of lesson 9, with an efficiency on the inflow.

A loop appears when a flow depends on a stock, such as sales that depend on the EVs already on the road.

  • Simulation steps such rules forward from given decisions; system dynamics, one method family in R3, writes the loops as equations.
  • Optimization chooses the decisions (lessons 10 to 13).
  • Which one a study needs is Researcher lesson R3.
Go deeperWhat this lab leaves out
  • Sizes: the map shows directions, not how much. Sizes need a model with data, prices and rules (Analyst and Modeler tiers).
  • Place and time: the same lever plays out differently in another region, market or year.
  • Many more links: a real system has far more parts and loops than fifteen cards and two diagrams.
  • Who decides: each ripple crosses the scales of F1, and trade-offs between goals are Analyst lesson A22.

Check yourself

Practice, about 10 minutes: four myths, then five questions.

Myth or fact? “If the inflow to a stock is falling, the stock is falling.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. A stock falls only when its outflow exceeds its inflow. In one test, business-school students new to these ideas showed a poor grasp of stocks and flows (Booth Sweeney and Sterman, 2000).

Myth or fact? “Holding CO₂ emissions steady holds the warming they cause steady.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Warming tracks cumulative CO₂ emissions, a stock: steady emissions keep adding to it. The IPCC finds net zero CO₂ is required to stabilize human-induced warming.

Myth or fact? “A reinforcing loop always means growth.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It reinforces whichever way it turns: fewer EVs, fewer chargers built, fewer EVs.

Myth or fact? “If people use a more efficient appliance more, the efficiency gain is wasted.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth: one review concludes that the direct rebound for household energy services in OECD (mostly high-income) countries should generally be less than 30%, so most of the saving is kept (Sorrell et al., 2009).

Prairie Town's battery is rated 25 MW and 100 MWh. Which number limits the stock?

  • 25 MW
  • 100 MWh
  • Both equally
Answer

100 MWh is the stock it can hold; 25 MW limits the flow in or out. Together: 4 hours at full power.

More rooftop solar → lower midday prices → less income per new panel → less new rooftop solar. Which kind of loop?

  • Reinforcing
  • Balancing
Answer

Balancing: one − link (more solar, lower prices), so the loop pushes back on its own growth.

Why does a heat wave squeeze spare capacity from both sides?

  • Demand rises while hot lines and some thermal plants deliver less
  • Prices rise, so plants sell less
  • Solar stops at noon
Answer

Demand rises for cooling while hot lines sag and carry less and some thermal plants, which make electricity from heat, lose output: both sides of the balance move against the grid.

A 300 MW data center connects, and no new supply comes with it. When does spare capacity shrink?

  • Years later, after a second event
  • At once, as soon as the load connects
  • Never: demand and supply are separate
Answer

At once: the extra demand uses up spare capacity when it connects. The map's waves trace causes in order; they are not always apart in time.

The map says bills “rise” after a carbon price. What would you need to say by how much?

  • More arrows on the map
  • A survey of opinions
  • A model with data: plants, fuel prices, the carbon price and the tariff
Answer

A model with data. The map shows directions; sizes come from models like the ones the Analyst and Modeler tiers build.

In the real world

  1. Measured: a study of 353 U.S. metro areas, 2011 to 2013, found effects running both ways, stronger on the car-buying side: chargers drew EV buyers, and EVs drew chargers (Li et al., 2017, abstract).
  2. Sized with data: real studies measure how strong such loops are; the lab only shows their shape.
