Read a real year of demand, then add solar and dig the duck curve

Data. Processed hourly profiles of the Southwest Power Pool (SPP) in 2025 from EIA-930, scaled to a fictional town whose busiest hour is 450 MW, as in lesson 0.
Repairs. Some demand and solar hours were rebuilt; see “What the data leave out” below.
Hours. Central time; 16:00 means 16:00 to 17:00.

What you just did, as diagrams

Lesson 0 ran one day like these hour by hour; lesson 1 showed that the area under a power curve is energy. Here the curve itself is the subject.

Daily shape
On 29 July 2025 demand rose from 298 MW at 04:00 to 450 MW at 16:00; on 21 January it had two humps, at 07:00 and 19:00.
Weekly rhythm
Weekend hours ran 4% below weekdays on average in 2025, and up to 9% lower around 07:00.
Seasons
July held the top hour; winter cold snaps made a second, lower season; April was the quietest month.
Load-duration curve
Sorted from highest to lowest, only 180 of 8,760 hours needed more than 90% of the peak.
Duck curve
Solar lowers midday net demand and steepens the evening climb that other plants must meet.
Go deeper · AnalystLoad factor, and the area under the curve

The load factor is average demand \(\bar d\) over peak demand \(d_{\max}\):

\[LF = \frac{\bar d}{d_{\max}} = \frac{282\ \text{MW}}{450\ \text{MW}} = 0.63 .\]

Each hour adds its demand times one hour of energy. Sorting changes the order, not the total: \(E = \sum_h d_h \times 1\ \text{h} \approx 2{,}469{,}131\) MWh, about 2,469 GWh, either way. Here \(d_h\) is demand in hour \(h\), in MW, and \(\sum_h\) adds all 8,760 hours.

The energy above a line \(L\) counts only the part of each hour’s demand over the line, \(\max(0,\ d_h - L) \times 1\ \text{h}\), in MWh. For \(L = 405\) MW it is 3,068 MWh, only 0.12% of \(E\): the top tenth of the peak holds very little of the year’s energy.

Go deeper · ModelerWhy models keep the hours in order
  • Sorting keeps how many hours each level of demand is needed.
  • Sorting loses which hour follows which. A battery’s charge, a plant’s ramp limit and the duck’s evening climb all depend on that order.
  • Models with memory keep enough of the order, in different ways, each with its own limits.
  • Next: lesson 9 lets storage fill the duck’s belly; lesson 11 solves a day hour by hour with a ramp rule; lesson 13 plans a year from representative weeks linked in calendar order.
Go deeperWhat the data leave out
  • One region only: the Southwest Power Pool in the central U.S., scaled down. Other regions peak at other hours and in other seasons.
  • For 699 hours of 2025 (about 8%) SPP's reported demand was implausible; those hours were rebuilt from SPP's own day-ahead forecast. The named days contain no rebuilt hours.
  • SPP's solar telemetry froze for 1,377 hours (about 16%); those hours were filled from the same hour on nearby days, and solar is set to zero after dark. The named days contain no filled hours.
  • SPP has little solar (0.8% of its 2025 generation), so the duck curve adds a hypothetical farm that follows SPP's solar shape.
  • The solar slider sets an illustrative maximum output, the farm's output in SPP's top solar hour of 2025; it is not a nameplate rating.
  • Real net demand subtracts wind as well as solar; here only solar is subtracted, to isolate its effect.
  • One year of data cannot show long-term growth.

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “Demand peaks at noon, when the sun is strongest.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. On 29 July 2025 the peak came at 16:00 to 17:00. Hot-day peaks often come after noon, as buildings hold heat and people come home.

Myth or fact? “Power plants are built for average demand.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. The grid as a whole must be able to meet the peak hour, with a margin: Prairie Town averages 282 MW but peaks at 450 MW.

Myth or fact? “Adding solar makes the evening easier for the other plants.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Solar fades while demand stays high, so the others must climb faster: on 30 May 2025, with 200 MW of solar, net demand's steepest three-hour climb after noon is 114 MW, against 20 MW without it.

180 of the year's 8,760 hours need more than 405 MW. What share of the year is that?

  • 21%
  • 2.1%
  • 0.2%
  • 18%
Answer

2.1%: 180 ÷ 8,760 = 0.021. Some resources are needed only in hours like these; how often each actually runs depends on the rest of the system.

Which change can raise Prairie Town's winter electricity demand by moving heating from gas to electricity?

  • More solar panels
  • Electric heat pumps replacing gas furnaces
  • LED lighting everywhere
  • A four-day work week
Answer

Heat pumps. A heat pump uses electricity to move heat, so heating moves from gas onto the grid. How much demand rises depends on adoption, weather and equipment.
