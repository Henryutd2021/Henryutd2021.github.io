Modeler checkpoint: formulate an unseen problem, find a defect, validate a result

About four hours on problems the course has not shown; worked formulations and code open when you submit

The Modeler checkpoint (CK-M) of Energy Systems, Step by Step: about four hours on problems the course has not shown. Formulate and solve a water utility’s pumping schedule as a linear program, or analyse a real year of grid data; find the planted defect in a supplied capacity-expansion model; validate one result against hand calculations or a published benchmark. Worked formulations and code open when you submit. Answers and times stay in your browser.

How this checkpoint works · about 240 minutes, no hints

  1. A · The brief (about 120 min). Choose a variant. Model: formulate the water brief as a linear program, solve it and read the solution. Empirical: analyse a real year of grid data.
  2. B · The planted defect (about 60 min). Find the one defect in a supplied model, say which check shows it, fix it and solve again.
  3. C · Validation (about 45 min). Check one of your results against an independent reference: hand calculations (model) or a published figure (empirical).
  4. Submit (about 15 min). Enter your results in the eleven tasks below. The worked formulations and code open when you submit task 11.

How it is marked, before you start

  • Proficient: at least 6 of the 9 auto-marked tasks right on the first try, including the defect's line (task 6), plus 3 of the 4 points of task 1 and 2 of the 3 of task 11, which you tick yourself.
  • Strong: 8 of 9 on the first try, the defect's line among them, and every written point.
  • Tolerances: each number task says how close counts as right.
  • Opening solutions early: a task whose solution you open before answering counts as not right. Opening the full worked solutions before task 11 makes the attempt practice, with no level.

Starter notebook (.ipynb) Dataset: town_year.json Briefs and model data (JSON, no answers)

The starter notebook holds both briefs' data and the supplied model, and gives no formulation and no answers. It runs in Google Colab or locally with Python 3.9 or later, NumPy and SciPy 1.9 or later. The empirical variant also needs town_year.json in the notebook's folder; its worked solution uses zoneinfo (on Windows, pip install tzdata; it falls back to 2025's clock rule without it).

Empirical variant · the brief

What can the town count on at its peaks?

Prairie Town's planners want to know how far its wind and solar farms can be counted on when the town needs power most. Report what the town can and cannot count on, and how far your results depend on the dataset's scaling.

  • Data: town_year.json (8,760 hours of 2025), the course's year file: the real 2025 hourly demand, wind and solar of the Southwest Power Pool grid (EIA-930), each as a shape in thousandths of its own highest 2025 hour
  • Metadata: the file's meta block gives the source, the time convention and how bad hours were filled
  • Scaling: as lesson 0: demand × 450 MW, the wind farm × 150 MW, the solar farm × 200 MW, so SPP's highest hour means a farm at its full size
  • Compare: each farm's output in the 100 hours of highest demand, and in the 100 hours of highest net load (demand minus wind and solar), with its average over the year
  • Ties: where hours tie, take the earlier one first

Part B · the supplied model, with one planted defect

The supplied model: what should Prairie Town build (wind, solar, a gas combined-cycle plant, a gas peaker) to meet demand on three representative days of 2025, each weighted by the days of the year it stands for? One planted defect.

On paper

  • Weights: w_d = the days of the year that day d stands for (121, 122, 122; they sum to 365).
  • Goal: min Σ_g A_g K_g + Σ_d w_d Σ_t [ (Σ_g c_g p_g,d,t) + V u_d,t ] ($ a year)
  • Balance: Σ_g p_g,d,t + u_d,t = D_d,t (MW, every day and hour)
  • Wind and solar: p_g,d,t ≤ a_g,d,t K_g (a: output per MW built, 0 to 1)
  • Gas plants: p_g,d,t ≤ K_g
  • Domains: K_g ≥ 0, p_g,d,t ≥ 0, u_d,t ≥ 0
The symbols, with their units
Symbol Meaning Unit
g a technology: wind, solar, gas combined-cycle (cc), gas peaker (pk) –
d, t a representative day, and an hour of it (t = 0 to 23, one hour each) –
w_d the days of the year that day d stands for days
A_g capital cost, already annualized $ per MW of capacity per year
K_g capacity built: a decision MW
c_g running cost $/MWh
p_g,d,t output in hour t of day d: a decision MW (held for one hour, so also MWh)
u_d,t demand left unserved: a decision MW (also MWh in its hour)
V the cost of unserved demand, $10,000 $/MWh
D_d,t demand MW
a_g,d,t output per MW built, 0 to 1 (wind and solar) MW per MW

Units of the goal: MW held for one hour × $/MWh = $ for each hour; summed over a day's 24 hours and multiplied by w_d days, $ a year, like A_g K_g.

In code (the starter notebook runs it)

  1. DAYS = {"winter": 121, "shoulder": 122, "summer": 122} # days of 2025 each day stands for
  2. weight = {d: DAYS[d] / 365 for d in DAYS} # share of the year
  3. cost = {"wind": 0, "solar": 0, "cc": 40, "pk": 100} # running cost, $ per MWh
  4. capital = {"wind": 100_000, "solar": 70_000, "cc": 80_000, "pk": 50_000} # $ per MW-year, annualized
  5. V = 10_000 # $ per MWh of demand left unserved
  6. minimize( sum(capital[g] * K[g] for g) + sum(weight[d] * (sum(cost[g] * p[g,d,t] for g) + V * u[d,t]) for d, t) )
  7. for d, t: sum(p[g,d,t] for g) + u[d,t] == demand[d][t] # balance, MW
  8. for g in ("wind", "solar"), d, t: p[g,d,t] <= avail[g][d][t] * K[g] # output per MW built
  9. for g in ("cc", "pk"), d, t: p[g,d,t] <= K[g]
  10. report(K, annual_cost=objective, demand_in_year=sum(weight[d] * demand[d][t] for d, t))
  • Costs: illustrative round numbers: capital already annualized ($ per MW of capacity per year); running costs as in lesson 0
  • Demand: the three real days' SPP shape × 450 MW
  • Wind and solar: output per MW built: the SPP shapes scaled to EIA's 2025 U.S. capacity factors (33.8% and 24.0%) with the course's scale method
  • Days: Thursday 16 January 2025 for December to March (121 days); Wednesday 2 April 2025 for April, May, October and November (122 days); Monday 15 September 2025 for June to September (122 days)

Model variant: the water tower as a linear program

Sets and inputs. Hours \(t = 0, \dots, 23\), each lasting \(\Delta t = 1\) h. Water use \(w_t\) (m³ in hour \(t\)); price \(\pi_t\) ($/kWh); \(k = 0.5\) kWh/m³; pump limit \(\bar Q = 900\) m³/h; tower limits \(\underline S = 800\) and \(\bar S = 4{,}000\) m³; start \(S_0 = 2{,}000\) m³; demand charge \(c = \$0.40\)/kW.

Decisions. Water pumped \(q_t\) (m³ in hour \(t\)), the tower’s level at the end of each hour \(s_t\) (m³), and the day’s highest draw \(P\) (kW).

\[\min \; \sum_t \pi_t\, k\, q_t \;+\; c\,P\]

\[s_t = s_{t-1} + q_t - w_t \;\;(s_{-1} = S_0), \qquad \underline S \le s_t \le \bar S,\]

\[0 \le q_t \le \bar Q\,\Delta t, \qquad P_t = k\,q_t / \Delta t \le P, \qquad s_{23} \ge S_0, \qquad P \ge 0 .\]

Units. \(\pi_t k q_t\) is $/kWh × kWh/m³ × m³ = $; \(cP\) is $/kW × kW = $. The hour’s draw \(P_t\) is its energy \(k q_t\) (kWh) divided by \(\Delta t = 1\) h, in kW; with \(\Delta t = 1\) h the code’s coefficients are the same numbers. The model has 49 variables, 24 tower rows, 24 draw rows and one end-of-day row. \(P\) plays the part of a built capacity in M3: one number every hour must stay under, paid once.

Empirical variant: the analysis and its benchmark

  1. Design first: a descriptive screen; metrics in % of each farm's size and in MW; 100% means SPP's highest 2025 hour, so the year's mean is not a capacity factor; the top hours sorted from the highest, the earlier hour first on ties.
  2. Load and check: 8,760 hours, no gaps; demand peaks at 450 MW and totals 2,469 GWh; wind and solar never above their sizes; the time index is hour-ending, from the file's start_utc.
  3. Compute: averages over the year and over the two sets of 100 hours.
Hours Wind (% of 150 MW) Solar (% of 200 MW) Both (MW)
The whole year 52.8% 23.4% 126.0
100 highest demand 58.1% 69.0% 225.1
100 highest net load 29.6% 4.3% 52.9
  • At the demand peaks (summer afternoons): both farms give more than their year averages.
  • At the net-load peaks (evenings, winter nights and mornings): wind gives 0.56 of its average, and solar almost nothing. These are the hours the rest of the fleet must cover.
  • Against the benchmark: the dataset's wind mean, 52.8% of its highest hour, is 19.0 percentage points above EIA's 2025 U.S. capacity factor of 33.8%; solar's 23.4% is close to EIA's 24.0%.
  • What the gap shows: a definition difference (shares of the highest hour, not of capacity), plus SPP's own fleet and wind. These data cannot split the two, so the comparison checks a definition; it does not validate the numbers.
  • Like for like, SPP's 2025 wind output (110.7 TWh, EIA-930) over its wind capacity as EIA counts it (33,385 MW, EIA-860M) is 37.9%: 4.1 points above the U.S. fleet's 33.8%. The definition explains 14.9 of the 19.0 points.
  • What survives, for the same hours: rescaled linearly to 33.8% (each hour × 0.338 ÷ 0.5275), wind's MW and percentages fall by one factor, so its ratio to its own average in these 100 net-load hours stays 0.56.
  • If the hours are picked again after rescaling, less wind raises net load in windy hours: 82 of the 100 hours stay, and the ratio becomes 0.71. Both are valid; say which you did.
  • What it leaves out: one weather year, outages, imports and an adequacy model; averages over top hours screen, they do not measure a capacity credit.

Part B: the defect explained

  1. Symptom: a gas peaker only, demand left unserved, and a year that holds 6,757 MWh of demand: about one day of the town's.
  2. Root cause, line 2: weight = DAYS[d] / 365 turns the days each representative day stands for into shares of the year. The paper says the weight is the number of days. With shares, the running costs of one average day meet a whole year of capital costs, so fuel looks almost free and capital-light plants win.
  3. The check that shows it: the year's total against an independent reference: the model's demand in the year, 6,757 MWh, against the town's 2,466 GWh. Units say the same: capital in $ a year, running costs in $ a day.
  4. The fix: weight = DAYS (121, 122, 122 days). Solve again.

Rubric

Your work, criterion by criterion

Criterion Not yet Developing Proficient Strong
Formulation or design No formulation, or rules in words only. Results The model or analysis does not run. It runs, but a key number is off by more than the tolerance. The key numbers within tolerance, each read from your own run. Also a marginal value (or a ratio) explained in words, with the rule or hours behind it.
The defect Not found. Found by trial, without a check that shows it. The line, the check that shows it, the fix and the fixed number. Also why the symptom looked the way it did (a cheap-capital plan, a year the size of a day).
Validation None. A comparison without a stated reference. One result against an independent reference, with the outcome stated. Also what the check cannot show, and which results depend on it.

The checkpoint's level

  • Proficient: at least 6 of the 9 auto-marked tasks right on the first try, including the defect's line (task 6); at least 3 of the 4 points of your formulation or design and 2 of the 3 points of your validation statement.
  • Strong: at least 8 of 9 on the first try, the defect's line on the first try, and every point of both written tasks.
  • Developing: at least 4 of 9 on the first try.
  • Not yet: anything else. The checkpoint shows readiness to start the Researcher tier; it certifies nothing.
  • Solutions opened early: a task whose worked solution you open before answering counts as not right on the first try. If the worked solutions above open before you submit task 11 (early, by an earlier attempt, or in the other variant), the attempt is practice and gets no level.

The written tasks are self-rated against their points. A teacher or mentor can rate them again with the table above, from the copied summary, which includes what you typed.
