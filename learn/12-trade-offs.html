What you just did, as diagrams

Dominated plan
Another plan costs no more and emits no more, and is better on one count: nobody should pick it.
Weighted sum
Score each plan by cost plus a carbon price times its CO₂; the lowest score wins.
Epsilon-constraint
Cap the CO₂ and find the cheapest plan under the cap; sweep the cap to trace the frontier.
Frontier
The plans that cannot get cheaper without getting dirtier, or cleaner without getting dearer.
Shadow price
Used as a carbon price, the cap's shadow price makes the cap's plan one of the cheapest choices.
Go deeper · AnalystThe limits of weighted sums
  • Dents. Plan C ($131.4M, 488 kt) lies above the straight line from B to D, so no carbon price picks it, though no plan beats it on both counts. Frontiers built from yes-or-no choices, such as building a plant or not, often have such dents.
  • Nearly straight pieces. Between 500 and 400 kt the slope barely changes ($43.4 to $44.0 a tonne), so prices from $43 to $45 move the plan from 541 kt to 389 kt.
  • Uneven spacing. Even steps in price give uneven steps along the frontier: up to $45 a tonne moves the plan from 792 kt to below 400 kt; from $45 to $220 it moves only to 100 kt.
  • Weights carry units. The weight in cost + w × CO₂ is a price in dollars per tonne; measure CO₂ in kilotonnes and the same number means something a thousand times larger.

Das and Dennis (1997) showed these drawbacks for weighted sums in general. The epsilon-constraint method avoids the first two by fixing one goal and optimizing the other.

Go deeper · ModelerThe cap's dual as a carbon price

The epsilon-constraint problem: the cheapest plan under a CO₂ cap.

\[\min_x \; c(x) \quad \text{subject to} \quad e(x) \le \varepsilon \;\; (\lambda), \;\; x \in X.\]

Symbol Meaning
\(x\), \(X\) a plan (what to build and how to run it), and every plan that meets the other rules
\(c(x)\), \(e(x)\) its yearly cost ($) and its CO₂ (tonnes)
\(\varepsilon\) the cap, in tonnes a year
\(\lambda\) the cap’s multiplier, or dual: its shadow price, in $ per tonne
\(\lambda^*\), \(c^*(\varepsilon)\) that multiplier at the best plan, and the best cost for cap \(\varepsilon\)
  • Duality. For a linear program, strong duality (a theorem that pairs every LP with a “dual” LP of prices) guarantees a \(\lambda^* \ge 0\) for which the cap’s best plan also minimizes the weighted sum \(c(x) + \lambda^* e(x)\). Other plans may tie with it, and a solver may return one of them.
  • Slope. Where the frontier is smooth, \(\lambda^* = -\,\mathrm{d}c^*/\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\); at a kink, \(\lambda^*\) lies between the slopes on either side.
  • Here. A 200 kt cap has \(\lambda^* = \$85.2\) per tonne; a $85.2 price with no cap lands at 199 kt and $129.3M.
  • Ties. On an exactly straight piece every plan ties at its slope; with integer decisions there is no such guarantee at all.

Mavrotas (2009) shows how to sweep \(\varepsilon\) efficiently. Next: A22 (five goals at once); upcoming: M6 (carbon in models).

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “The cheapest plan is always the right choice.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Here plan A stays the pick only while CO₂ is priced below $53 a tonne. The frontier shows what each tonne avoided costs; people decide what that is worth.

Myth or fact? “A carbon price and a CO₂ cap can lead to the same plan.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. A 200 kt cap has a shadow price of $85.2 a tonne; that price, with no cap, lands at 199 kt, next to the cap's plan.

Myth or fact? “Every plan that is not dominated can be picked by some carbon price.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Plan C is not dominated, yet it sits in a dent between B and D, so no price picks it.

Plan B costs $119.4M a year and emits 521 kt; plan D costs $136.8M and emits 249 kt. Above what carbon price does D score lower than B?

  • About $64 a tonne
  • About $17 a tonne
  • About $273 a tonne
  • D never scores lower
Answer

About $64: D costs $17.4M more and emits 272 kt less, and 17.4 million ÷ 272,000 tonnes ≈ $64 a tonne.
