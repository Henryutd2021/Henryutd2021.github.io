Capacity factor: why is 1 MW not always 1 MW?

Run three 100 MW plants for a year and count what they make

Energy made: the 24 outputs add up to 676 MWh. Capacity factor: 676 ÷ (100 × 24) = 28.2%. The night hours count too.

The power curve of an illustrative 3 MW wind turbine

  • Below 3 m/s (cut-in): no electricity.
  • 3 to 12 m/s (rated): output follows the cube of the wind speed; doubling a speed within this range gives eight times the output.
  • 12 to 25 m/s (cut-out): output holds at 3,000 kW.
  • Above 25 m/s: the turbine shuts down to protect itself.

Go deeper · AnalystCapacity factor depends on the period and the place

Every fleet’s capacity factor changes from month to month. U.S. averages in 2025 (EIA, Table 6.07.B):

Fleet Lowest month Highest month
Wind 23.1% (September) 43.1% (March)
Solar 13.1% (December) 31.9% (June and July)
Nuclear 81.0% (October) 99.2% (December)

Nuclear dips in spring and fall, the seasons when many reactors stop to refuel.

  • Availability is not capacity factor. A solar farm can be ready all night and still make nothing.
  • Curtailment lowers it. A wind farm turned down by the grid makes less than its weather allowed (lesson 0).
  • Sites differ. The U.S. averages hide windy plains and sunny deserts; Prairie Town’s shapes come from one region.

Go deeper · ModelerThe power curve, from physics to measurements

Power in the wind. Through a rotor, the wind carries \(P_\text{wind} = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho A v^3\).

Symbol Meaning The lesson’s turbine
\(\rho\) air density, kg/m³ 1.225 kg/m³, sea level
\(A\) area the blades sweep, m² 7,854 m², a 100 m rotor
\(v\) wind speed, m/s 12 m/s, its rated speed
\(P_\text{wind}\) power in the wind, kW 8,313 kW

What the turbine captures. It makes 3,000 kW at 12 m/s, so its power coefficient is \(C_p = 3{,}000 / 8{,}313 = 0.36\). No rotor can capture more than \(16/27 \approx 0.593\) of the wind’s power: the Betz limit.

The lesson’s clean power curve, output \(P\) in kW at wind speed \(v\) in m/s:

\[ P(v) = \begin{cases} 3000 \times (v/12)^3 \text{ kW} & 3 \le v < 12 \text{ m/s}\\ 3000 \text{ kW} & 12 \le v \le 25 \text{ m/s}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

A measured curve. Real 10-minute averages scatter around a curve: gusts, air density and the turbine’s own controls move each one.

The chart shows a 1.5 MW research turbine that NREL tested in 2011, beside a clean curve built the same way for it: the cube law at its best \(C_p\), 0.52, capped at 1,500 kW.

Go deeperWhat this lesson leaves out
  • The wind and solar shapes are whole regional fleets, smoother than any single farm.
  • Wind and solar are reshaped so that each month matches the U.S. average and the best hours reach full size.
  • The seasons are national; the hours are those of one region.
  • The nuclear plant's outage and the turbine are illustrative, not a real plant or model.
  • Air density changes with temperature and altitude, which shifts a real power curve.
  • The clean curve has no spread: a real turbine's output scatters around it (the measured curve above).

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “A 100 MW wind farm makes 100 MW most of the time.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In 2025, U.S. wind farms made 33.8% of what they would have made at full size, and the cube law makes their output swing with every change in the wind.

Myth or fact? “A solar farm with a 24.0% capacity factor is broken three-quarters of the time.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. At night and under clouds it works as designed: the sun, not the machine, sets most of the gap.

Myth or fact? “Doubling the wind speed doubles a turbine's output.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. On this lesson's turbine it gives eight times the output (2 × 2 × 2), but only while both speeds lie between cut-in (3 m/s) and rated (12 m/s); then output holds at rated power until cut-out (25 m/s).

A 50 MW solar farm made 105,120 MWh in a year of 8,760 hours. What was its capacity factor?

  • 24.0%
  • 48.0%
  • 12.0%
  • 100%
Answer

24.0%: 105,120 ÷ (50 × 8,760) = 0.240. Leaving out the night hours would wrongly raise it: capacity factor counts every hour. Using 100 MW would halve it.

At the 2025 U.S. average of 91.1%, how much energy does 1 MW of nuclear make in a year?

  • 8,760 MWh
  • 7,980 MWh
  • 911 MWh
  • 91.1 MWh
Answer

7,980 MWh: 1 MW × 8,760 h × 0.911. The full 8,760 MWh would need every hour at full size.
