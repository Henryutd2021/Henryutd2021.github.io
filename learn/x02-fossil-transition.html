Fossil systems and carbon: what each does today, and the open question

System Today A number Open question
Gas networks and LNG Carry gas to power plants, industry and about 60% of U.S. homes; LNG ships it overseas. 15.1 Bcf a day of U.S. LNG exports in 2025, 26% of the world's Who pays for the pipes as customers leave?
Gas power plants 41% of U.S. electricity from utility-scale plants (1 MW or larger) in 2025. 0.96 pounds of CO₂ per kWh of electricity generated, burning only (2023) A bridge or a lock-in?
Coal plants and mines 17% of U.S. utility-scale electricity in 2025; 512.5 million short tons (2,000 pounds each) mined in 2024. 8.5 GW planned to retire in 2025, 2.6 GW retired When does a plant close, and who carries what is left?
Oil About 38% of U.S. primary energy in 2025, used mostly for transport. Transport took two-thirds of the petroleum used in 2022 How exposed is an importer to one sea route?
Carbon capture and storage Captures CO₂ at a chimney or gas-processing plant and stores it underground; CO₂ that is used instead may be released again. Capture capacity of about 50 million tonnes a year worldwide (2025) Can it grow against about 38 billion tonnes of energy CO₂ a year?
Carbon removal Takes CO₂ already in the air into trees, soils, rock or minerals. Stored for decades to 10,000 years or longer, by method How long does it stay, and how is it measured?

Six ways to remove carbon (IPCC, 2022)

Method Stores carbon in Readiness (1 to 9) Cost range per tonne CO₂ Potential, billion tonnes CO₂ a year Measuring it
Direct air capture with storage (DACCS) Rock underground: 10,000 years or longer 6 $100 to $300 5 to 40 Count the CO₂ stored, less emissions from the energy and water it uses.
Bioenergy (energy from plant matter) with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) Rock underground: 10,000 years or longer 5 to 6 $15 to $400 0.5 to 11 Count the CO₂ stored, less emissions and land carbon lost growing the biomass.
Enhanced weathering (crushed rock on fields) Minerals: 10,000 years or longer 3 to 4 $50 to $200 2 to 4 The carbon ends up as bicarbonate dissolved in water or as carbonate minerals; uptake in real soils is still poorly quantified.
Biochar (charred plant matter in soil) Soils: decades to centuries (IPCC's figure gives one timescale per storage medium; how long char itself lasts depends on how it is made) 6 to 7 $10 to $345 0.3 to 6.6 Count the carbon in the char, less emissions from making it.
New and restored forests (afforestation and reforestation) Trees and soils: decades to centuries 8 to 9 $0 to $240 0.5 to 10 Carbon in growing trees; wildfire, disease or pests can reverse it.
Soil carbon in croplands and grasslands Soils: decades to centuries 8 to 9 $−45 to $100 0.6 to 9.3 IPCC: the net addition per hectare is very small and hard to monitor.
  • Readiness: 1 = basic principles described, 9 = proven in operation.
  • Cost: U.S. dollars per tonne of CO₂, IPCC's author-judgment ranges from studies in different dollar years, not adjusted to one year. A low end below zero is an estimated net saving.
  • Potential: billions of tonnes of CO₂ a year that the literature finds possible, not today's scale.
  • Why BECCS can count as removal: plants take CO₂ from the air as they grow; storing that carbon after the biomass is used can give net removal, once other emissions and land-carbon losses are counted.
  • Coal retirements in 2025: 8.5 GW planned, 2.6 GW retired (31% of the plan).
  • Capture capacity worldwide: about 50 million tonnes of CO₂ a year (IEA, December 2025). Compared with 2025's energy CO₂, about 38 billion tonnes, that capacity is about 0.13%, even running full all year.

Two worked examples (illustrative teaching numbers)

  • No pipe retired: The whole network stays, so the customers who remain share its whole assumed cost.
  • With 30% of customers gone, each remaining customer pays $714 a year for the network, against $500 today (illustrative).
  • Pipes to leavers retired: Half of the leavers' share of the cost is avoided by retiring the pipes that served them.
  • With 30% of customers gone, each remaining customer pays $607 a year for the network, against $500 today (illustrative).
  • An early closure: Closing 25 years after opening leaves 37.5% of the building cost unrecovered: 15 of its 40 years of even recovery still to come (illustrative).

Is gas a bridge or a lock-in? The strongest case on each side

  • A bridge: Burning gas for power emitted 0.96 pounds of CO₂ per kWh of electricity generated in 2023 against coal's 2.31 (EIA, utility-scale, burning only).
  • A bridge: Gas plants already make 41% of U.S. utility-scale electricity, on networks that exist today.
  • A lock-in: Gas networks last decades: EIA reported in March 2024 that about half of the long-distance gas pipeline network went in during the 1950s and 1960s.
  • A lock-in: Leaks before burning narrow the gap: one study put U.S. oil and gas methane at 2.3% of gas produced in 2015 (Alvarez et al. 2018, abstract).
  • What would tell them apart: How many hours new gas plants run in later decades, and measured leak rates (F11 counts leaks as CO₂e).

What you just did, as diagrams

Roles today
Gas made 41% and coal 17% of U.S. utility-scale electricity in 2025; oil was about 38% of primary energy.
A plan is an assumption
Owners planned to retire 8.5 GW of coal in 2025; 2.6 GW retired.
Networks have fixed costs
Fewer customers can mean a higher share each, unless pipes are retired or costs are recovered another way.
Capture is not removal
Capturing fossil CO₂ and storing it durably can cut emissions; CO₂ that is used instead may be released again. Removal takes back CO₂ already in the air.
Removal differs by method
Rock and minerals store carbon for 10,000 years or longer, trees and soils for decades to centuries; readiness, cost range and measurement differ too.

Where we stop

This side topic stops at roles and open questions

Fossil systems and carbon removal enter the course as what each does for the system today, how to read a plan by its assumptions, and the questions still open; no forecasts.

This side topic covers

  • What gas networks, LNG, coal plants and mines, oil, carbon capture and carbon removal do today.
  • Reading a retirement plan or a gas-network scenario by its assumptions.
  • Removal methods compared by storage time, readiness, cost range and how they are measured.

Left to specialists

  • Forecasts of when plants close or how much gas is used.
  • Engineering design of pipelines, capture plants or storage sites.
  • Advocacy for or against any fuel or method.

Read next, three rungs

  1. Start here. EIA's plain-language pages on how the United States uses natural gas, with the latest data. EIA, Natural gas explained: Use of natural gas, last updated 31 October 2024, read 7 November 2026
  2. Go further. IPCC's assessment of carbon removal, its methods and its roles; its Chapter 6 covers energy systems. IPCC, Sixth Assessment Report, Working Group III, Chapter 12: Cross-sectoral perspectives, 2022, read 7 November 2026
  3. Go deep. Plant-by-plant trackers of coal plants, gas plants and pipelines worldwide, to test a plan against the record. Global Energy Monitor, its coal and gas trackers, cited by name, updated through the year
Go deeper · AnalystReading a retirement or gas-network scenario by its assumptions
  • Who wrote it, and when? An owner’s filing, a regulator’s order and a modeler’s scenario answer different questions; date each one.
  • Plan, scenario or forecast? A retirement plan states an owner’s intent; a scenario states “if these assumptions hold”; neither is a prediction.
  • What is held fixed? Demand, fuel prices, rules and the network’s cost: change one and the result moves.
  • Who carries the cost? Owners, customers or taxpayers, as set by regulators and contracts.
  • What would change it? An emergency order, a price swing or a new rule: in 2025, EIA counted many coal retirements delayed under emergency orders.
Go deeperWhat this lesson leaves out
  • Forecasts of plant closures, gas demand or removal at scale.
  • Engineering of pipelines, LNG terminals, capture plants and storage sites.
  • Carbon markets, credits and the rules for counting removals in an inventory.
  • Methods IPCC assesses beyond the six here, such as ocean-based removal and wetland restoration.

Check yourself

Practice, about 10 minutes: four myths, then three questions.

Myth or fact? “Capturing CO₂ at a coal plant removes CO₂ from the air.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Stored durably, the captured share of the plant's own CO₂ stays out of the air; IPCC: capture applied to fossil fuel use is not removal, since it takes no CO₂ out of the air.

Myth or fact? “Carbon stored in new forests stays there for good.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. IPCC puts trees and soils at decades to centuries, and wildfire, disease or pests can reverse it.

Myth or fact? “LNG is natural gas cooled until it is a liquid about 600 times smaller.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. EIA: it is cooled to −260 °F, and the liquid is 600 times smaller than the gas.

Myth or fact? “When owners plan to retire a coal plant, it closes that year.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In 2025 owners planned 8.5 GW and 2.6 GW retired; 4.8 GW was delayed and 1.1 GW canceled (EIA).

EIA: in 2023 burning coal for power emitted 2.31 pounds of CO₂ per kWh of electricity generated and burning gas 0.96. About what share of coal's is gas's?

  • About 20%
  • About 40%
  • About 60%
Answer

About 40%: 0.96 ÷ 2.31 = 0.416, or 42%. Burning only: leaks before burning are not counted.

A network costs $500 a year per customer and no pipe can be retired; a quarter of the customers leave. What does each remaining one pay for it (illustrative)?

  • $500
  • $667
  • $625
Answer

$500 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $500 ÷ 0.75 = $667. The network's total cost has not changed; it is shared by fewer customers.

In IPCC's table, which method stores carbon for 10,000 years or longer but has the lowest readiness, 3 to 4?

  • Direct air capture with storage
  • Biochar
  • Enhanced weathering
Answer

Enhanced weathering: crushed rock reacts with CO₂, which ends up as bicarbonate in water or in carbonate minerals. Its readiness is 3 to 4, and its uptake in real soils is still poorly quantified (IPCC).
