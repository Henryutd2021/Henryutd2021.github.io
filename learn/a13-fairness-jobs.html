Who pays and who benefits? Energy burden, fairness and jobs

Split a Prairie City plan’s cost across household groups three ways and watch energy burden move, then count jobs and the tax base when a coal county’s plant retires

A13 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: recover a Prairie City plan’s yearly cost per kWh, by a fixed charge and by income, and read each household group’s energy burden; see that moving a cost is a transfer, not a new cost; then count job-years against permanent jobs, gross against net jobs and the county’s tax base when a coal plant retires. Every output is a screening estimate.

  • Ridge County (fictional). Its coal plant retires: 1,150 jobs on site, about 1,840 with suppliers and spending (multiplier 1.6).
  • Job-years against jobs. 2,000 MW of wind and solar at $1,500 per kW give about 7,200 job-years of building gross (5,760 net of 20% displacement), and about 192 permanent jobs gross, 154 net.

Go deeper · AnalystReal costs, transfers and real income-based designs
  • A real cost uses something up: wires, equipment, labor, fuel. A transfer moves money between people: taxes, rebates, a charge moved from one group to another.
  • Count each once. Add real costs to judge a plan and read transfers to see who gains and who loses; adding a transfer to a cost counts it twice.
  • Real designs. California’s regulator adopted a fixed charge in three income tiers for its large utilities (income-based charge; CPUC Decision 24-05-028, May 2024).
Go deeper · AnalystWhere real job and tax coefficients come from
  • Input-output models trace what each industry buys from the others. NREL’s JEDI models turn spending into jobs this way and count a construction job as one full-time year; they report gross jobs only.
  • Multipliers. BEA’s RIMS II sells regional multipliers for output, earnings and employment; new ones came out on 5 May 2026.
  • Baselines. BLS’s Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages counts jobs and wages by county and industry; DOE’s USEER counts energy jobs by technology.
  • Taxes. Rates, assessments (the value set for tax) and abatements (tax reductions) are set by each state and county: read the local rules, not a national average.
Go deeper · ModelerThe accounting layer, as a model you can reuse

Take groups \(g\) with \(n_g\) households, income \(y_g\), use \(e_g\) (kWh) and home energy spending \(s_g\). A yearly cost \(C\) becomes a charge per household

\[c_g = \frac{C\,e_g}{\sum_h n_h e_h} \ \text{(per kWh)}, \qquad c_g = \frac{C}{\sum_h n_h} \ \text{(fixed)}, \qquad c_g = \frac{C\,y_g}{\sum_h n_h y_h} \ \text{(by income)}\]

  • In words. Per kWh, each group pays the cost’s share of the kWh it uses; fixed, every household pays the same; by income, each pays the share of the city’s income it earns.
  • Burden after: \((s_g + c_g)/y_g\). Every design gives \(\sum_g n_g c_g = C\), so the difference between two designs sums to zero: a transfer.
  • Jobs. With direct (on-site) new permanent jobs \(J_{\text{new}}\), direct jobs lost \(J_{\text{lost}}\), multiplier \(m\) and displacement \(d\): net permanent jobs \(= m\,(J_{\text{new}}(1 - d) - J_{\text{lost}})\).
  • Job-years over \(T\) years from retirement, building first for \(b\) years: \(m\,[(B + (T - b)\,J_{\text{new}})(1 - d) - T\,J_{\text{lost}}]\), with \(B\) the direct building job-years (spending in the county \(\times\) direct job-years per $M).
  • Reuse. The page’s accounting is one small shared component, whopays.js, that the course’s city sandbox and capstone can call with a plan’s cost and its changes in use.
  • An energy model does not produce these outcomes by itself: it gives costs, energy and MW; an accounting layer like this one turns them into who pays and who gains.
Go deeperWhat this lesson leaves out
  • How households and firms respond to prices, and how people move, retrain or start firms.
  • What the plan delivers to each group: cleaner air, lower bills from efficiency, reliability.
  • Who gets a say in the decision, as well as who pays.
  • Field surveys, interviews and causal studies of labor markets: the social-science methods behind real estimates.

Practice · about 30 minutes on your own

  1. The per-kWh rate by hand. Prairie City's households use 1,162,169,159 kWh a year. What rate recovers $24M?

    Answer

    $24M ÷ 1,162,169,159 kWh = $0.0207, about 2.07¢ a kWh.

  2. A fixed charge against income. Split $24M equally over 101,410 households. How much does it add to the burden of a household earning $13,000?

    Answer

    $237 a year each; 236.66 ÷ 13,000 = 1.82%, so +1.8 points.

  3. The same share of income. Prairie City's households earn $8,428M a year in all. Charged by income, what share of its income does every household pay?

    Answer

    $24M ÷ total household income $8,428M = 0.285%; +0.3 points for every group.

  4. Group, then read. Households below 80% of area median income: how does their merged burden compare with the poorest group's, before the plan?

    Answer

    Merged: total spending ÷ total income = 6.1%; the 0–30% group alone: 14.9%. The merged average hides the tail.

  5. Job-years against jobs. $1,500 per kW for 2,000 MW, 30% spent locally, 5.0 direct job-years per $M, multiplier 1.6. Compare with 1,840 permanent jobs lost over 20 years.

    Answer

    $3,000M × 30% = $900M; × 5.0 = 4,500 direct job-years; × 1.6 = 7,200 job-years in all (gross), against 1,840 × 20 = 36,800 job-years lost.

  6. The county's budget. Plant value $3,000M, new farms $3,000M with 50% taxable, rate 2.0%. What changes for the county's budget, and what does this model leave open?

    Answer

    $60M lost, $30M gained: −$30M a year for the county's budget, the mines' taxes held fixed. The payments use up nothing; what a smaller budget does to services, and to the region, is not estimated.
