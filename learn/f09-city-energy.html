What does a city run on, and who controls what?

Prairie City's year of energy, 2025, in GWh (1 GWh = 1 million kWh)

Prairie City is fictional; its numbers are illustrative.

Sector Takes in Useful energy Counted as lost
Buildings: homes, shops, offices and public buildings 3,994 GWh 3,586 GWh 408 GWh (10%)
Transport: cars, buses and trucks 3,517 GWh 1,059 GWh 2,458 GWh (70%)
Industry and farms (the industrial park, the farms) 1,481 GWh 552 GWh (and 591 GWh of hydrogen) 338 GWh (23%)
Data-center campus, one large load kept apart 1,076 GWh 1,076 GWh not estimated (its electricity counts as useful)

Convention: for computers, appliances and lights, the electricity delivered counts as useful; their own losses are not estimated, so a sector's loss share is not its efficiency.

Boundary: the fertilizer plant's flow stops at the hydrogen its gas reformers make, at its higher heating value; turning that hydrogen into ammonia is outside this count.

  • Carriers. Electricity 3,476 GWh; Gasoline 3,167 GWh; Natural gas 2,859 GWh; Diesel 444 GWh; Propane 125 GWh.
  • Electricity. Rooftop solar makes 0.9% of it; the rest comes from the SPP grid, and 164 GWh is lost in the city's wires.

What you just did, as diagrams

Carriers to sectors
A city buys gas, fuels and electricity, and uses them in buildings, transport and industry; buildings take the most, 3,994 GWh.
Useful and lost
Engines lose most of their fuel as heat: transport turns 30% of what it takes in into motion.
A convention
For computers, appliances and lights, the electricity delivered counts as useful; their own losses are not estimated, so a sector's loss share is not its efficiency.
The power sector
Prairie City makes 0.9% of its electricity; its accounting starts where the regional grid's power enters its wires.
Who decides
For a Kansas city served by an investor-owned utility, the state regulator approves rates; federal rules set equipment standards and govern interstate markets.
The city and owners
The city adopts its energy code and issues permits; owners choose their equipment.
Typical, not universal
A city that owns its utility sets its own rates; many states adopt one energy code for the whole state, and states organize their utilities differently.
Go deeper · AnalystWhere a city's count starts
  • Final energy is what the city buys at the meter or the pump; useful energy is the warmth, motion and light it gets.
  • A convention. For appliances, lights and computers the electricity delivered counts as useful, so their own losses are not estimated; for heat and vehicles, the heat or motion delivered counts.
  • The boundary. Power plants on the regional grid are outside the city: their losses appear in a regional or national count, not here.
Go deeper · AnalystWho decides, in a little more detail
  • Rates. An investor-owned utility asks its state commission to change rates; the commission reviews its costs and approves the rates and terms.
  • Supply. In states without retail competition, as in Kansas, utilities may build, own and run power plants; states with retail competition leave generation to competing suppliers.
  • The grid. A regional operator such as SPP runs the bulk grid and the wholesale market under FERC’s rules.
  • Codes. The U.S. has no national energy code: states or local governments adopt one, and local permit offices enforce it.

Check yourself

Practice, about 10 minutes: three myths, then four questions.

Myth or fact? “Prairie City's council alone decides which power plants make its electricity.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In Kansas the utility owns plants and signs supply contracts, the state regulator reviews their costs, and SPP's market decides which plants run across the region; the city shapes its own use and permits.

Myth or fact? “The city council sets the electricity rate every home pays.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth for an investor-owned utility: in Kansas, the state's commission approves the rates. Fact where the city owns its utility.

Myth or fact? “Most of the energy a car burns moves the car.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Prairie City's transport takes in 3,517 GWh and turns 1,059 GWh into motion; the rest leaves as heat.

Prairie City's homes buy 1,155 GWh of electricity, 1,376 GWh of gas and 99 GWh of propane. About what share of what they buy is gas?

  • A quarter
  • Half
  • Three quarters
Answer

About half: 1,376.2 ÷ 2,630.0 = 52%. Their heat pumps also draw 138 GWh of free outdoor-air heat; counting it, 1,376.2 ÷ 2,767.7 = 50%.

Who approves the rates of Kansas's investor-owned electric utilities?

  • The city council
  • FERC
  • The Kansas Corporation Commission
Answer

The Kansas Corporation Commission, the state regulator; FERC regulates interstate transmission and wholesale sales.

Which body regulates interstate transmission and wholesale power sales?

  • FERC
  • The state regulator
  • The utility
Answer

FERC, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.

Prairie City's meters take 3,280.7 GWh of grid electricity a year (rooftop solar aside), and the wires lose 5% as much again (set for teaching). How much is lost?

  • About 16 GWh
  • About 164 GWh
  • About 1,640 GWh
Answer

3,280.7 × 0.05 = 164.0 GWh, about the flow to distribution losses in the Sankey.
