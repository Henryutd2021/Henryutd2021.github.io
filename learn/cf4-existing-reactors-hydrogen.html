Can existing reactors earn more by making hydrogen and providing grid services?

Follow one study through six parts, then run a toy plant that sells power, makes hydrogen and holds reserves at prices you set

1 · The puzzle

The pressure

Nuclear power supplies reliable low-carbon electricity and high-temperature heat, but existing plants face growing pressure to operate more flexibly in markets with rising renewable penetration.

The option

A nuclear plant can do more than sell electricity around the clock.

  • With high-temperature electrolysis, part of its electricity and steam can make hydrogen;
  • with hydrogen storage and a battery, it can shift its output and offer ancillary services to the grid.

The open question

Coupling nuclear reactors with hydrogen production through high-temperature electrolysis may improve flexibility and create additional value, yet its economic potential remains uncertain.

So can an existing plant earn more by making hydrogen as well as power?

2 · The question in one sentence

Can existing U.S. nuclear power plants improve their economics and operating flexibility by adding high-temperature electrolysis, hydrogen storage and batteries, and by selling energy, hydrogen and ancillary services?

Question type: prescriptive (what should be added, and how should it run?).

3 · Scale and boundary

Inside
  • 42 operating U.S. reactors in seven ISO/RTO markets, each facing its own hourly energy and ancillary-service prices for 2024 (8,760 hours; February 29 is left out);
  • a high-temperature electrolyzer, hydrogen storage and a battery added to each plant;
  • one year of hourly operation turned into a lifetime cash flow.
Assumed throughout

a hydrogen production credit of $3/kg for ten years, counted in hydrogen revenue.

Left out
  • price feedback from large-scale adoption, because every plant takes 2024 prices as given;
  • re-optimized operation after the hydrogen credit expires, because the dispatch is held fixed.

4 · Decisions, goal and rules, in words

Decisions

in every hour,

  • how much of the plant's electricity and steam goes to the electrolyzer;
  • when the electrolyzer runs and at what load;
  • what goes into and out of hydrogen storage and the battery;
  • grid sales and purchases;
  • and the bids of the turbine, electrolyzer and battery in the market's ancillary services.
Goal

the largest yearly profit (energy, hydrogen and ancillary-service revenue minus operating and annualized capital costs), with the hydrogen production credit counted in hydrogen revenue.

Rules
  • turbine and electrolyzer efficiency follow piecewise-linear curves;
  • the electrolyzer keeps minimum up and down times, ramp limits and a minimum stable load, pays a start-up cost and degrades with operation and start-ups;
  • ancillary-service bids must fit within each unit's headroom and ramp limits.

5 · Data and method

  • Seven U.S. markets price energy and ancillary services differently, and each of 42 reactors faces its own prices.
  • The study links an hourly mixed-integer optimization of turbine, electrolyzer and storage dispatch, for 42 reactors in seven U.S. ISO/RTO markets using 2024 prices, to a lifetime cash-flow model.
  • The hourly dispatch model co-optimizes electricity sales, hydrogen production, storage and ancillary services under each market's products, and is run for every reactor.
DetailWhat the equipment can do, and the two cases
  • Electrolyzers and turbines are not ideal: efficiency varies with load, starts wear equipment, and ramping is limited.
  • The model represents these limits, so flexibility estimates respect them.
  • Each plant is run two ways: business-as-usual operation without any modification for hydrogen production, and a retrofit with high-temperature electrolysis, hydrogen storage and a battery.
DetailFrom one year to a lifetime
  • The study treats an existing plant's capital as already spent (a sunk cost); the retrofit's cash flow adds the capital cost of the new hydrogen and battery equipment.
  • A discounted cash-flow analysis turns the optimized year into net present value, internal rate of return, payback and levelized costs, with component-level capital costs and scheduled replacements.
  • Electricity and hydrogen prices are taken as given (price-taking): the study evaluates plant operation against observed 2024 prices, and price feedback from wide adoption is stated as a limit and left to future work.
DetailSensitivity runs

Sensitivity runs on a representative reactor vary the electrolyzer's capacity bound and the level of electricity prices.

6 · What came out, and where it holds

Operated as electricity-only baseload plants at 2024 market prices, without capacity payments or the 45U credit, nearly all of the plants studied had negative net present value.

With hydrogen integration and the assumed hydrogen production credit ($3/kg for ten years), a majority of the plants studied reached positive net present value, even without the 45U credit.

With the assumed hydrogen credit, integrated operation added output flexibility: flexible output (the range of a plant's hourly output to the grid) expanded by more than 115 MW for most of the plants studied.

Ancillary-service revenue was a few percent of total revenue, which includes the hydrogen credit.

From: Li et al. (2026), Nature Communications.
Holds when
  • 2024 prices, taken as given;
  • modeled ancillary-service participation parameters;
  • the assumed hydrogen credit ($3/kg for ten years), with the dispatch held fixed after the credit expires;
  • an existing plant's capital treated as already spent;
  • no capacity payments and no 45U credit in the baseload case.
Leaves out
  • capacity-market revenues;
  • price feedback from large-scale adoption.

The toy's assumptions
  • Illustrative: the plant makes 1,000 MW of electricity in every hour, in both cases.
  • Its fuel and upkeep are the same either way, and its own capital is already spent (a sunk cost), so none of them changes the comparison.
  • Electricity only: the electrolyzer makes 25 kg of hydrogen from each MWh of the plant's electricity it takes (40 kWh per kg), sold at an assumed $2.00 per kg, fixed: $50 per MWh.
  • The electrolyzer's capital, as an annual equivalent, and its upkeep: $400 per MW of electrolyzer per day, paid whether it runs or not.
  • Reserves, simplified: one product, paid per MW held for an hour (per MW-hour), never called; turning the electrolyzer down sends its electricity to the grid, so its load is the reserve.
  • Two different measures: the range of output to the grid over the day, from its lowest hour to its highest, and the reserve, the MW held for the grid in one hour.
  • Left out: the steam a high-temperature electrolyzer also takes; the electrolyzer's minimum load, start-ups, ramp limits and wear; hydrogen storage and a battery; prices that move when the plant changes what it sells.
  • With no storage and no start-ups, each hour is decided on its own: one hour's choice does not change the next.

Go deeper · ModelerThe toy's day as a small optimization

The toy’s symbols, for each hour \(t\) of the day:

  • \(p_t\): the grid price, in dollars per MWh sold; \(P\): the plant’s output, in MW; \(E\): the electrolyzer’s size, in MW.
  • \(v = k\,\pi_H\): what a MWh earns as hydrogen, \(k\) kg per MWh at an assumed \(\pi_H\) dollars per kg.
  • \(q\): the reserve price, in dollars per MW held for an hour; \(c\): the electrolyzer’s daily cost, in dollars per MW.
  • Decisions: \(g_t\) MW to the grid, \(h_t\) MW to the electrolyzer, \(r_t\) MW held as reserve.
  • \(\Delta t = 1\) h, the length of each step: a price per MWh times MW times \(\Delta t\) gives dollars. It equals one here because every step is one hour.

\[\max \; \sum_t \left( p_t\,g_t + v\,h_t + q\,r_t \right) \Delta t \; - \; c\,E\]

Rule, in every hour Why
\(g_t + h_t = P\) the plant runs at full power
\(0 \le h_t \le E\) the electrolyzer’s size
\(0 \le r_t \le h_t\) only the electrolyzer’s load can be handed back to the grid
\(g_t \ge P - E \ge 0\) follows from the first two
  • The best choice: \(h_t = E\) when \(v + q > p_t\), else \(h_t = 0\) (on a tie the toy sells); \(r_t = h_t\), or \(q = 0\) and \(r_t = 0\) with no reserves.
  • Against selling all the power, the day changes by \(E \sum_t \max(0,\; v + q - p_t)\,\Delta t - c\,E\); the plant’s own costs appear on both sides, so they cancel.
  • Each hour stands alone only because the toy has no storage, start-ups or ramp limits; adding them links the hours into a mixed-integer program, as in M2 and M11.

Check yourself, then try it

Myth or fact? “Whether the electrolyzer pays depends on what the plant itself cost to build.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. That cost is already spent and the same either way, so it drops out: in the toy at its defaults, only the power sold, the hydrogen and reserves earned, and the electrolyzer's own $80,000 a day change.

Myth or fact? “A plant running at full power can still hold reserves if it runs an electrolyzer.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. Turning the electrolyzer down sends its power to the grid instead, so its load is headroom: in the toy at its defaults, 200 MW × 20 hours × $8 per MW-hour = $32,000.

Try it yourself

About 5 minutes, in the toy above.

Without JavaScript the toy's controls do not run: compare its default table above with the worked answers below.

Start each one from the defaults: press Reset (price level ×1.0, reserve price $8 per MW-hour, electrolyzer 200 MW, reserves offered).

  • Set the price level to ×0.6, then to ×1.4. In how many hours does the electrolyzer run, and how does the day compare with selling all the power?
  • Turn reserves off. In how many hours does the electrolyzer run now, and how much of the day's gain came from reserves?
  • Set the electrolyzer to 0 MW, then to 400 MW. What range of output to the grid does each give, and what happens to its cost?
  • Pick 13:00, then 19:00 on a chart. Where does each MWh go, and why?

With open code

Optional Modeler extension, about 30 minutes; needs Python 3.10 or later on your computer (lesson B2).

Try it yourself: the framework's minimal example compares business as usual with the integrated system over two synthetic weeks, solved with HiGHS.

Generate synthetic inputs, then run the hourly operation again with energy prices 15% higher, or with ancillary services in dispatch mode, and compare the revenues and the flexibility.

Its numbers say nothing about any real plant, market, policy or year.

Set it up first: clone the repository and install it as its README's Quick start says; run the commands in the repository's folder.

  • python examples/minimal.py
  • nuclear-h2 synthetic my_inputs/ --storage
  • nuclear-h2 run my_inputs/ --out-dir outputs/base
  • nuclear-h2 run my_inputs/ --price-factor 1.15 --out-dir outputs/price_up
  • nuclear-h2 run my_inputs/ --as-mode dispatch --out-dir outputs/dispatch

Each run writes revenue_musd_per_yr.csv and summary.json into its --out-dir folder. Compare the revenue tables, and grid_output_range_mw under "flexibility" in summary.json: the range of output to the grid, the toy's measure too.
