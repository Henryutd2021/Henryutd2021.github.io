The twelve cards

Twelve regions, one structure: the system, who decides, the main challenges, what researchers ask, key numbers with their sources, and the date they were checked. The cards describe; none ranks regions or judges a policy.

How to read the numbers

Power and energy
GW, MW and kW measure power, a rate; TWh, GWh and kWh measure energy, an amount. 1 TWh = 1,000 GWh = 1 billion kWh.
Fossil, non-fossil
Fossil fuels are coal, oil and gas; non-fossil is everything else, nuclear included.
Renewable, clean
Renewables are hydro, wind, solar, bioenergy and geothermal; Ember's 'clean' adds nuclear to them.
Non-conventional renewable
A category in Chilean law that leaves out large hydro.
Prices
Hawaii's are in US cents per kWh.
Dates
Each citation gives the source's date and the year its data describe; each card ends with the day its numbers were checked.

United States

Focus of this card: regional grid · interconnection queues · large-load adequacy

System structure
  • Seven independent grid operators (ISOs and RTOs) run wholesale markets; elsewhere vertically integrated utilities own plants and wires, as in much of the Southeast and West.
  • Three main grids, the interconnections, weakly linked to one another.
  • Gas is the largest source of electricity; demand is rising again with data centers, manufacturing and electrification.
Institutions
  • FERC (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission): wholesale markets and interstate transmission.
  • NERC (North American Electric Reliability Corporation): reliability standards.
  • DOE (Department of Energy) and EIA, its statistics agency: research, policy and statistics.
  • State utility commissions: retail rates.
  • ISOs and RTOs: grid operation and markets.
Main challenges
  • A long interconnection backlog, and who pays for new transmission.
  • Adequacy, enough supply in nearly every hour, as large loads arrive and older plants retire.
  • Extreme weather.
Distinctive research questions
  • How much capacity can flexible large loads save?
  • Do the cluster-study reforms after FERC Order 2023 shorten the time to operation?
  • What does transmission between regions buy in extreme cold?

Verified key numbers

  • About 2,061 GW seeking interconnection at the end of 2025: 1,312 GW of generation and about 749 GW of storage. (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, May 2026; data: end of 2025)
  • Wind and utility-scale solar made 17% of 2025's generation; 19% with small-scale solar. (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 20 March 2026; data: 2025)
  • Data centers used 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023 (176 TWh). (U.S. Department of Energy, 20 December 2024; data: 2023)

The Plains (SPP region)

Focus of this card: regional grid · wind · winter adequacy

Prairie Town's real-data home: the course's hourly shapes come from this grid in 2025, before its western expansion.

System structure
  • A regional transmission organization (RTO): it runs the bulk grid and the wholesale market for its members.
  • One market since 1 March 2014, the Integrated Marketplace: day-ahead and real-time energy, reserves and congestion rights.
  • In the Eastern Interconnection it sits between MISO to the east and ERCOT to the south.
  • Since 1 April 2026 its RTO services also reach parts of seven western states, in the Western Interconnection.
Institutions
  • SPP: reliability coordination, scheduling, transmission planning and the wholesale market.
  • FERC, the federal regulator: granted SPP its RTO status in 2004.
  • SPP's Market Monitoring Unit: an independent monitor that reports on the market each year.
  • Members: generation-and-transmission cooperatives, municipal systems, investor-owned utilities, independent transmission companies and power producers.
Main challenges
  • Congestion and wind curtailment: average hourly curtailment rose from 136 MW in 2019 to 1,097 MW in 2023.
  • Winter adequacy and fuel supply: in February 2021 SPP cut regional demand twice, the first region-wide curtailments in its history.
  • In 2025, rising gas prices, load growth and resource adequacy concerns.
Distinctive research questions
  • How much does new transmission cut wind curtailment and congestion?
  • How should capacity be accredited when cold weather takes out many plants and their fuel at once?
  • How should wind forecast uncertainty enter the day-ahead commitment?

Verified key numbers

  • 161 members in all or part of 17 states, about 732,086 square miles; among them 26 generation-and-transmission cooperatives, 18 municipal systems and 16 investor-owned utilities. (Southwest Power Pool, updated October 2026; data: October 2026, after the western expansion)
  • Wind made 36.7% of the 300,382 GWh produced in 2025, more than coal (29%) or gas (25.1%). (Southwest Power Pool, updated October 2026; data: 2025, before the western expansion)
  • Wind capacity 35,934 MW at the end of 2025. (Southwest Power Pool, 27 May 2026; data: end of 2025, before the western expansion)
  • Peak 58.0 GW on 27 July 2026, a record; the earlier peak was 56.0 GW on 21 August 2023. (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 15 October 2026; data: 27 July 2026, after the western expansion)
  • February 2021: demand cut by about 1.5% for 50 minutes on 15 February and about 6.5% for about three hours on 16 February. (Southwest Power Pool, undated; data: February 2021)

Checked 7 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

European Union

Focus of this card: regional grid · market coupling · flexibility

System structure
  • Unbundled: grid operation is kept apart from generation and sales, through separate companies or independently run subsidiaries.
  • Each price zone has its own price; coupled markets link the zones a day ahead and within the day.
  • Dense interconnection between countries.
  • Nuclear power, led by France, with wind and fast-growing solar.
  • Flat demand overall, with clusters of data-center load.
Institutions
  • European Commission (DG ENER, its energy department): energy law and policy.
  • ACER, the EU Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators: links the national regulators.
  • ENTSO-E, the European network of transmission system operators: grid plans.
  • National regulators and transmission system operators (TSOs); Eurostat for statistics.
Main challenges
  • Congestion, and the cost of redispatch: paying plants to change their output so flows fit the lines.
  • Negative prices, and falling solar capture prices.
  • Security of supply after 2022.
  • Slow permitting of grids.
Distinctive research questions
  • How does the design of price zones affect redispatch?
  • Which mix of storage, demand response and interconnection cuts negative-price hours most?
  • Which connection rules fit clusters of data-center load?

Verified key numbers

  • Renewables made 47.5% of gross electricity consumption in 2024 (Austria 90.1%, Malta 10.7%). (Eurostat, 14 January 2026; data: 2024)
  • Wind and solar made 30% of 2025's electricity, above fossil fuels at 29%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, 22 January 2026; data: 2025)
  • Data centers used 23% of Ireland's metered electricity in 2025 (5% in 2015). (Central Statistics Office, July 2026; data: 2025)

China

Focus of this card: nation · curtailment · ultra-high-voltage lines

System structure
  • State-owned grid companies; dispatch largely by province.
  • Spot markets moving toward a unified national market; market pricing for new wind and solar since 2025.
  • Ultra-high-voltage (UHV) lines carry power from west to east; coal still leads generation.
Institutions
  • NDRC (National Development and Reform Commission) and NEA (National Energy Administration): planning, prices and regulation.
  • State Grid and China Southern Power Grid.
  • China Electricity Council; the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS).
  • Beijing and Guangzhou power exchanges.
Main challenges
  • Rising curtailment of wind and solar.
  • Barriers to trade between provinces.
  • Coal plants retrofitted for flexibility, and capacity payments.
Distinctive research questions
  • How do provincial spot designs affect how much wind and solar is used?
  • Which mix of ultra-high-voltage lines, storage and flexible coal keeps curtailment low at least cost?
  • How does market pricing change where wind and solar are built?

Verified key numbers

  • 317 GW of solar added in 2025; about 95% of available solar output and 94% of available wind output was used, not curtailed (this is not a capacity factor). (National Energy Administration of China, 12 February 2026; data: 2025)
  • Solar capacity 1,286 GW at the end of July 2026, passing coal at 1,285 GW. (National Energy Administration of China, 11 September 2026; data: end of July 2026)
  • Fossil fuels made 58% of 2025's electricity; wind and solar 22%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, read 2 November 2026; data: 2025)

India

Focus of this card: nation · evening peak · distribution-company finances

System structure
  • State distribution companies (DISCOMs) buy power under long-term contracts.
  • One national grid running at one frequency (synchronous).
  • Coal makes most of the electricity, while most capacity is now non-fossil.
  • The evening peak, after sunset, is the hardest time to meet demand.
Institutions
  • Ministry of Power and the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy (MNRE).
  • Central Electricity Authority (CEA): national electricity planning.
  • CERC (Central Electricity Regulatory Commission) and state commissions: regulation and tariffs.
  • Grid-India (operator), CTUIL (transmission planning) and SECI (renewable tenders).
Main challenges
  • The finances of distribution companies.
  • The evening peak in a solar-heavy system.
  • Transmission and storage for the Rajasthan and Gujarat renewable zones.
Distinctive research questions
  • How much storage does each step in solar's share need?
  • How do solar-powered farm feeders and time-of-day tariffs reshape load?
  • Does market coupling outperform bilateral contracts?

Verified key numbers

  • Non-fossil capacity 300.50 GW, over 54% of about 552 GW, on 31 July 2026. (Press Information Bureau, 9 August 2026; data: 31 July 2026)
  • Fossil fuels made 73% of 2025's electricity; solar 9.4%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, read 2 November 2026; data: 2025)

Checked 2 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

Japan and South Korea

Focus of this card: regional grid · import dependence · links between regions

System structure
  • Japan: ten utility areas, nine of them interconnected; Okinawa's is separate.
  • Japan's east runs at 50 Hz and its west at 60 Hz, joined by limited frequency converters.
  • Japan: retail open to competition, a power exchange (JEPX) and a capacity market.
  • South Korea: one network and retail company (KEPCO) and a cost-based pool (KPX).
  • Both are isolated systems, without links to other countries, that import their fuels; nuclear plays a large role in both.
Institutions
  • Japan: METI and its energy agency (ANRE), OCCTO (coordination between the areas), the Nuclear Regulation Authority, JEPX (Japan Electric Power Exchange).
  • South Korea: the government's energy ministry, KEPCO (Korea Electric Power Corporation) and KPX (Korea Power Exchange).
Main challenges
  • Dependence on imported fuel.
  • Curtailment and weak links between regions (Kyushu, Jeju).
  • Finding sites for offshore wind.
Distinctive research questions
  • What would more 50/60 Hz converter capacity save?
  • How should South Korea plan southwest offshore wind together with lines to Seoul?
  • LNG storage or batteries as a hedge for supply security?

Verified key numbers

  • Japan, 2025: fossil fuels 67% of electricity, solar 10%, nuclear 9%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, read 2 November 2026; data: 2025)
  • South Korea, 2025: fossil fuels 60% of electricity; wind and solar 7%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, updated 22 April 2026; data: 2025)

Nordic countries

Focus of this card: regional grid · hydro and wind · bidding zones

System structure
  • Nord Pool bidding zones: each zone has its own price.
  • Since October 2024 the Nordic day-ahead market uses flow-based coupling, which limits trades by the flow on each line.
  • Dense HVDC links.
  • Hydro in Norway and Sweden, wind in Denmark; Sweden and Finland also run nuclear plants.
  • New industrial, hydrogen and data-center loads; district heating in many cities.
Institutions
  • Grid operators: Statnett, Svenska kraftnät, Fingrid, Energinet.
  • National regulators.
  • Nord Pool: the power exchange.
  • Statistics Norway (SSB) and Eurostat: statistics.
Main challenges
  • North-south congestion and price gaps.
  • Dry-year risk for hydro.
  • Little inertia, and new large loads.
Distinctive research questions
  • How has flow-based coupling changed price spreads?
  • How should the value of stored water be modeled as wind grows?
  • What flexibility can district heating give?

Verified key numbers

  • Norway, 2025: 161.8 TWh generated, hydro 89.9%, a record net export of 22.8 TWh. (Statistics Norway, May 2026; data: 2025)
  • Renewables' share of gross electricity consumption in 2024: Sweden 88.1%, Denmark 79.7%, Finland 54.3%. (Eurostat, 14 January 2026; data: 2024)

Checked 2 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

Gulf states

Focus of this card: nation · cooling peaks · power and water

System structure
  • Vertically integrated state utilities; single state buyers sign contracts with independent power producers and power-and-water plants.
  • Oman runs a spot market; the GCC Interconnection Authority links all six states.
  • Gas and oil make most electricity, with large solar tenders.
  • The UAE also runs a nuclear plant.
Institutions
  • Saudi Arabia: Ministry of Energy, the regulator SERA, Saudi Electricity Company.
  • UAE: Ministry of Energy and Infrastructure, DEWA (Dubai Electricity and Water Authority), EWEC (Emirates Water and Electricity Company).
  • Qatar's KAHRAMAA, its electricity and water utility; Oman's regulator APSR.
  • The Gulf Cooperation Council Interconnection Authority (GCCIA).
Main challenges
  • The value of oil burned for power that could be sold.
  • Summer peaks driven by cooling.
  • Subsidized tariffs, and power and water plants that run together.
Distinctive research questions
  • How should power and water be planned together with solar and storage?
  • How much peak can district cooling and thermal storage shave?
  • What is reserve sharing through the GCCIA worth?

Verified key numbers

The surveyed figures for Saudi Arabia and the UAE are held back: their source gives no data year. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics: Saudi Arabia: country profile and United Arab Emirates: country profile, updated 22 April 2026)

Sub-Saharan Africa

Focus of this card: microgrid and local · energy access · power pools

System structure
  • Mostly vertically integrated state utilities; Nigeria unbundled in 2013, and South Africa's Eskom is unbundling.
  • Thin regional power pools, groups of neighboring countries that trade power: Southern (SAPP), West (WAPP), Eastern (EAPP) and Central African (CAPP).
  • Hydro in many countries, coal in South Africa; diesel and off-grid solar where the grid does not reach.
Institutions
  • National utilities and regulators.
  • The regional power pools.
  • The African Development Bank, the World Bank and AUDA-NEPAD, the African Union's development agency.
Main challenges
  • The rural access gap.
  • Utility finances, and load shedding: planned, rotating power cuts.
  • Climate risk to hydro; a high cost of capital.
Distinctive research questions
  • Where is grid extension, a mini-grid or a solar home system the least-cost way to connect people?
  • What is regional trade worth when river flows vary?
  • Which tariffs make mini-grids viable?

Verified key numbers

  • 655 million people worldwide without electricity, over 560 million of them in Sub-Saharan Africa. (World Bank and the custodian agencies, 24 June 2026; data: 2024)
  • South Africa, 2025: 83% of electricity from fossil fuels; wind and solar 13%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, updated 22 April 2026; data: 2025)

Checked 2 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

Latin America

Focus of this card: nation · hydro and climate risk · curtailment

System structure
  • Brazil: a hydro-dominated national grid (the SIN, its National Interconnected System), cost-based dispatch, auctions plus a free market.
  • Chile: liberalized, with cost-based dispatch on one long north-south grid.
  • Mexico: the state utility CFE (Federal Electricity Commission) dominates after the 2025 reform.
Institutions
  • Brazil: MME (the energy ministry), the regulator ANEEL, the operator ONS, the planner EPE, the trading chamber CCEE.
  • Chile: the energy commission CNE and the operator CEN.
  • Mexico: SENER (the energy ministry), the operator CENACE, CFE.
  • OLADE, the Latin American Energy Organization.
Main challenges
  • Drought risk to hydro.
  • Curtailment and transmission bottlenecks in northern Chile.
  • Investment uncertainty in Mexico.
Distinctive research questions
  • How should reservoirs be run together with wind and solar under drought?
  • Which storage and transmission cut Chile's curtailment most cheaply?
  • How do compensation rules for rooftop and other distributed generation shift costs?

Verified key numbers

  • Brazil, 2025: hydro 52% of electricity, wind and solar 27%, clean sources 89%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, updated 30 October 2026; data: 2025)
  • Chile: 20,483.5 MW of non-conventional renewable capacity out of 39,104.4 MW installed, November 2025. (Coordinador Eléctrico Nacional, December 2025; data: November 2025)
  • Mexico, 2025: 74% of electricity from fossil fuels; wind and solar 13%. (Ember, a compiler of official statistics, updated 20 May 2026; data: 2025)

Checked 2 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

Small island systems

Focus of this card: microgrid and local · imported fuel · low inertia

System structure
  • One integrated utility per island.
  • Small, isolated grids with little inertia.
  • Imported diesel and fuel oil; high shares of rooftop solar.
Institutions
  • Hawaii's Public Utilities Commission and State Energy Office.
  • CARICOM's Energy Unit and CCREEE, the Caribbean's center for renewable energy and efficiency.
  • The Pacific Community, its center PCREEE and the Pacific Power Association.
  • IRENA's SIDS Lighthouses initiative, for small island developing states.
Main challenges
  • Volatile fuel costs.
  • Stability at very high shares of inverter-based power.
  • Storm resilience.
Distinctive research questions
  • How much grid-forming capacity does a grid that runs almost entirely on inverters need?
  • Which mix of solar, storage and diesel holds up under fuel-price and storm risk?
  • How should rooftop export tariffs work once midday solar exceeds load?

Verified key numbers

  • Hawaii's average retail price was 38.00 US cents per kWh in 2024, the highest of any state; the U.S. average was 12.94 cents. (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 10 November 2025; data: 2024)
  • Hawaii had the highest average residential bill in the U.S. in 2024: $213 a month. (U.S. Energy Information Administration, 12 May 2025; data: 2024)

Checked 2 November 2026: each number was read on its source that day.

Australia

Focus of this card: regional grid · rooftop solar · minimum demand

System structure
  • The National Electricity Market (NEM): one wholesale market across Queensland, New South Wales and the ACT, Victoria, South Australia and Tasmania.
  • Western Australia runs its own Wholesale Electricity Market.
  • Millions of rooftop solar systems; grid-scale batteries growing fast.
Institutions
  • AEMO (Australian Energy Market Operator): independent market and system operator and system planner for the NEM and Western Australia's market.
  • The Clean Energy Regulator: runs the Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme for rooftop systems, which ends in 2030.
  • State governments and distribution network businesses: emergency backstops that can turn rooftop solar down.
Main challenges
  • On sunny, mild days, typically in autumn and spring, rooftop solar supplies much of what customers use, so demand on the grid's large generators falls very low.
  • Keeping enough essential system services as large spinning generators run less.
  • Evening peaks (4 pm to 9 pm), now partly met by batteries.
Distinctive research questions
  • How can the grid stay secure when operational demand, the demand left for its generators, falls very low?
  • How do home and grid-scale batteries reshape evening peaks and prices?
  • Which services replace what large spinning generators gave the grid?

Verified key numbers

  • Renewables made 44.3% of NEM generation in July to September 2026, a record for that quarter (41.8% a year earlier). (Australian Energy Market Operator, October 2026; data: July to September 2026)
  • NEM-wide minimum operational demand (the demand left for the grid's generators after rooftop and other distributed solar) fell to an all-time low of 9,422 MW on Sunday 20 September 2026. (Australian Energy Market Operator, October 2026; data: 20 September 2026)
  • Rooftop and other distributed solar averaged 3,203 MW in July to September 2026, a record for that quarter. (Australian Energy Market Operator, October 2026; data: July to September 2026)
  • 4,562,198 small-scale solar systems installed across Australia, 30,917,812 kW of rated output, as of 30 September 2026 (all of Australia, not only the NEM). (Clean Energy Regulator, last updated 20 October 2026; data: 30 September 2026)
