How much can shifting load to cleaner hours cut emissions, and when?

Average and marginal emission factors, and when moving electricity use to cleaner hours lowers CO₂

Case file CF6 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: average and marginal emission factors, and when moving electricity use to cleaner hours lowers CO₂.

1 · The puzzle

Grid electricity is cleaner in some hours than in others. If some electricity use moves into the cleaner hours, without using less in total, how much CO₂ does that save?

Words first: attributed emissions are a share of the grid's CO₂ assigned to a user; intensity is CO₂ per MWh; a window is the stretch of time within which use may move.

2 · The question in one sentence

How much do the emissions attributed to electricity consumption fall when load is re-timed according to hourly average grid emission intensity across U.S. regions, and which strategies suit which types of grid?

Question type: descriptive (how much?), with a prescriptive part (which strategy suits which grid?).

3 · Scale and boundary

  • Inside: U.S. grid regions, every hour of 2023, and each region's load, moved within windows of a month, a quarter or a year.
  • Left out: which plants actually change output when load moves, forecast errors, and the cost of moving load beyond a penalty.

4 · Decisions, goal and rules, in words

  • Decisions: how much each hour's load moves up or down.
  • Goal: the lowest attributed emissions over the window, plus a penalty for each adjusted hour.
  • Rules: the window's total use does not fall; no hour moves beyond the allowed range, such as ±5%.

5 · Data and method

  • A mixed-integer load-regulation model, solved for each window with EIA's hourly data for 2023.
  • Regions are grouped by renewable share, the diversity of their generation and how much their intensity swings; the window, the range and the penalty are varied.

6 · What came out, and where it holds

Longer optimization windows and wider adjustment ranges increased the reduction in attributed emissions, most in renewable-rich and diverse grids.

In grids with diverse generation resources, expanding the adjustment range from ±5% to ±8% can boost carbon reductions from 1.19% to 1.64%.

In the reported scenarios, reductions in attributed emissions were on the order of one to two percent of regional totals or less.

Regions with low intensity fluctuation were more sensitive to the cost of regulating load.

Reductions are computed under average-emission-intensity accounting with perfect foresight; they are not estimates of the consequential (marginal) system response.

Approved excerpt of: Li et al. (2025), Cell Reports Sustainability.

Holds when: each hour keeps its average intensity, and the whole window is known in advance. Leaves out: which plants respond.

Two factors, two questions

Factor How it is built The question it answers Moving 10 MWh from 06:00 to 03:00
Average (t CO₂ per MWh) the hour's CO₂ ÷ the hour's use How much of the grid's CO₂ is attributed to my use? −0.3 t
Marginal (t CO₂ per MWh) the CO₂ of the plant that serves one more MWh What do the plants emit differently if I change my use? 0.0 t

Shifting cuts what the plants emit when it moves use away from a dirtier marginal plant, toward spare wind and sun or a cleaner plant. The marginal plant is lesson 8's idea.

Slide the adjustment range

  1. Each hour of the grid has an average emission intensity.
  2. The model moves part of the load from high-intensity hours to low-intensity hours in the same window, within an allowed range, and keeps total use unchanged.
  3. Attributed emissions fall by the load moved times the intensity gap between those hours.
  4. Because the range is limited, the reduction is modest; it grows with a wider range and a longer window.

Where the result holds

It holds when
  • emissions are counted at each hour's average intensity;
  • each window is known in advance (perfect foresight);
  • each window's total use does not fall;
  • hourly EIA data for U.S. grid regions in 2023.
It leaves out
  • the grid's consequential response: which plants actually change output (the marginal view);
  • forecast errors;
  • the cost of moving load, apart from a penalty on each adjusted hour.

From the approved excerpt of Li et al. (2025), Cell Reports Sustainability.

Go deeper · ModelerMarginal emission factors from a model
  • In a dispatch model, an hour’s marginal emission factor is the change in total CO₂ when that hour’s demand grows by one MWh.
  • The toy reads it off each hour’s dispatch; a linear program finds it by solving again with one more MWh.
  • It holds for a small change only, and it jumps between plants from hour to hour, so real estimates come from dispatch models or from statistics on years of hourly data.
  • Putting carbon in the model: caps, prices, frontiers and procurement rules (Modeler lesson M6) takes this further.

Check yourself, then try it

Myth or fact? “Moving use into an hour with a lower average CO₂ per MWh always lowers what the plants emit.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In Prairie Town, moving 10 MWh from 06:00 to 03:00 lowers the average-factor count by 0.3 t, yet the plants emit the same: the combined-cycle plant is on the margin in both hours.

Myth or fact? “Moving use out of a gas hour into an hour when wind or sun is being spilled lowers what the plants emit.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact, up to the spilled amount.

At 14:00 Prairie Town spills 25 MWh of wind and sun, so 10 MWh moved there from 20:00 cut the peaker's CO₂ by 5.5 t; beyond the spill, the next plant serves it.
