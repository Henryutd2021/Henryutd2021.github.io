What should be built? Capacity expansion with representative periods, validated

Plan on a few weeks, then test the plan on every hour of two weather years

Modeler lesson M3 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: write a capacity-expansion linear program that chooses what to build and how to run it, choose representative weeks and their weights, link a battery’s charge across the weeks and see what goes wrong without the link, then re-run the chosen plan on all 8,760 hours of 2025 and of 2024 and measure the error; a Python notebook rebuilds the model.

Build it in Python · notebook lab, about 60 minutes, then 120 minutes of practice

The same model in scipy.optimize.linprog(method="highs") with sparse matrices. assert cells check your own plans against the reference: the weeks, LP sizes, sizes built, costs, CO₂ and unserved energy.

Hours short and the largest shortfall are printed, not asserted: other schedules of the same cost can spread a shortfall differently.

  1. Download the notebook and the four data files into one folder (in Colab, upload them with the folder icon).
  2. Open it in Google Colab (File → Upload notebook) or locally with Python 3, NumPy and SciPy 1.9 or later.
  3. Run all cells (the full-year optimum takes seconds to a minute), then try the six practice tasks at the end.

Go deeper · ModelerThe expansion LP in algebra

For each representative week \(p\) and hour \(h = 1 \dots 168\), at least yearly cost:

\[\min \; \sum_i f_i K_i + \sum_p w_p \sum_{h} \big(r_\mathrm{cc}\, g_{p,h} + r_\mathrm{pk}\, q_{p,h}\big)\]

\[ \begin{aligned} a^{w}_{p,h} K_\mathrm{wind} + a^{s}_{p,h} K_\mathrm{solar} + 0.911\,K_\mathrm{nuc} + g_{p,h} + q_{p,h} + d_{p,h} - c_{p,h} &\ge D_{p,h} && \text{(demand)}\\ g_{p,h} \le K_\mathrm{cc}, \quad q_{p,h} \le K_\mathrm{pk}, \quad c_{p,h} + d_{p,h} &\le K_\mathrm{bp} && \text{(sizes)}\\ s_{p,h} = s_{p,h-1} + 0.85\,c_{p,h} - d_{p,h}, \quad s_{p,0} = 0, \quad \ell_p \le s_{p,h} &\le u_p && \text{(battery, within week } p)\\ S_{i+1} = S_i + s_{k(i),168}, \quad S_{53} = S_1, \quad 0 \le S_i + \ell_{k(i)}, \quad S_i + u_{k(i)} &\le K_\mathrm{be} && \text{(link, calendar weeks } i)\\ \textstyle\sum_p w_p \sum_h \big(0.329\, g_{p,h} + 0.484\, q_{p,h}\big) &\le \text{cap} && \text{(CO}_2\text{)}\\ K_i,\ g_{p,h},\ q_{p,h},\ c_{p,h},\ d_{p,h},\ S_i \ge 0, \quad s_{p,h} \text{ free in sign}, \quad \ell_p \le 0 &\le u_p && \text{(domains)} \end{aligned} \]

Each step \(h\) lasts one hour, so a power held for one step (MW) is that much energy (MWh). Sizes, outputs, charge and discharge cannot be negative; the level \(s_{p,h}\) is measured from the week’s start, so it can be.

The weight of a modeled hour: \(w_p = n_p \times 8{,}760 / 8{,}736\), where \(n_p\) is the number of calendar weeks it stands for and the factor spreads the 24 hours of 31 December. A typical week standing for 13 weeks: \(13 \times 8{,}760 / 8{,}736 = 13.04\); an extreme week stands for itself.

Symbol Meaning Unit
\(K_i\) size built; battery power \(K_\mathrm{bp}\) MW
\(K_\mathrm{be}\) battery energy MWh
\(f_i\) yearly cost of one unit built: capital recovery at 4% real plus fixed O&M; for a plant with constant output, also its fuel and variable O&M $ per MW-year (per MWh-year)
\(w_p\) hours of the year one modeled hour of week \(p\) stands for h
\(g, q\) gas combined-cycle and peaker output MW
\(r_\mathrm{cc}, r_\mathrm{pk}\) running cost of gas $/MWh
\(c, d\) battery charge and discharge MW
\(s_{p,h}\) battery level in week \(p\), relative to the week’s start (may be negative) MWh
\(\ell_p, u_p\) the lowest and highest \(s_{p,h}\) in week \(p\) MWh
\(S_i\) battery level at the start of calendar week \(i\); \(k(i)\) is the representative week standing for it MWh
\(a^w, a^s\) wind and solar output per MW built MW per MW
\(0.911\) nuclear output per MW built, the same in every hour MW per MW
\(D\) demand MW
\(0.329, 0.484\) CO₂ per MWh of gas output (heat rate × 0.05291 t per MMBtu): operating CO₂ only t/MWh

Cyclic storage replaces the chain \(S_{i+1} = S_i + s_{k(i),168}\) with \(s_{p,168} = 0\) for every \(p\): each week ends where it starts, from a start level of its own. No link drops the chain only: each week may start at any level, whatever the week before left. Fixing every \(K_i\) and running all 8,760 hours as one period, with unserved demand allowed at an illustrative $10,000 per MWh, gives the full-year check.

Go deeper · ModelerHow the weeks are chosen
  • Typical weeks. Each season’s weeks (by the month of the week’s middle) are grouped by k-medoids, \(k\) = 1, 2 or 3. Each group’s medoid is the real week closest, hour by hour, to the others in its group: squared differences of demand (as a share of the peak), wind and solar output per MW.
  • Scaling. A typical week is scaled so that its group keeps its demand energy, and its wind and solar energy to within 0.5%: no hour may exceed a plant’s size.
  • Extremes. The week of the year’s peak demand, of the peak of demand minus reference wind and solar output, and of the least wind are kept as they were, each standing for itself.
  • Same weather year. Demand, wind and sun come from the same hours, so their correlation survives.
  • Outages are left out of the planning LP: they belong to an adequacy study.

Measure Definition 4 + 3 extreme weeks
Estimate error (weeks’ optimal cost) ÷ (full-year optimal cost) − 1 −3.0%
Cost gap (plan’s spending on the full year) ÷ (weeks’ estimate) − 1. Spending: plants and fuel; no shortage penalty, no cap in the re-run +1.1% on 2025
Unserved energy demand not met when the plan is re-run, in MWh, hours and largest shortfall 108 MWh in 8 hours
CO₂ against the cap the plan’s full-year CO₂ ÷ cap − 1 +8.7% on 2025 (217 kt), +7.3% on 2024 (215 kt)
  • Four typical weeks leave 879 MWh unserved in 54 hours of 2025, spread over 12 weeks.
  • The second year. At 2025’s yearly demand energy, 2024’s demand peaks at 462 MW, against 450 MW in 2025.
  • What they cannot tell you. Even the plan optimal on all of 2025 leaves 235 MWh in 17 hours of 2024 unserved.
  • Adequacy is judged over many weather years with plant outages, in a Monte Carlo study (A5), by measures such as the loss-of-load expectation (LOLE).
  • LOLE is the expected number of days a year with a shortage of any size or length. One criterion is 0.1 days a year, “1 day in 10 years” (PJM Manual 20A, §1.3, June 2026): an expectation, not a promise of one outage a decade.
Go deeperWhat this model leaves out
  • Plant outages and reserve margins: adequacy studies treat them over many weather years (A5).
  • Start-up costs and minimum run times: on/off decisions make a mixed-integer program (M2).
  • Transmission and regions: the town is one bus with no imports.
  • Several years of investment, retirements and residual value: a pathway model (M13).
  • Uncertainty when building: here the weather is known when the sizes are chosen (M8 chooses them before it is known).
  • Costs that change with time: every cost is constant within the year modeled.

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “More representative weeks always give a better plan.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. 8 typical weeks leave 750 MWh unserved on the full year; 4 typical + 3 extreme leave 108 MWh. Which weeks matters as much as how many.

Myth or fact? “Leaving out the battery's link between weeks costs only a little accuracy.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. In an illustrative what-if with storage energy at $20 per kWh (not a forecast or a sourced cost):

  • the plan with no link builds 2,685 MWh of battery;
  • re-run on the full year, it emits 247 kt against a 200 kt cap;
  • each week could start charged, for free.

Myth or fact? “A plan that serves every hour of one full year is adequate.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. The plan optimized on all 8,760 hours of 2025 is short in 17 hours of 2024. Adequacy needs plant outages and many weather years.

The seven weeks estimate $129.28M a year; the full-year optimum costs $133.25M. What is the estimate error, as this lesson defines it?

  • −3.0%
  • +3.1%
  • 0.970
  • −$3.97M
Answer

−3.0%: 129.28 ÷ 133.25 − 1 = −0.030, with the reference in the denominator. +3.1% reverses the comparison: the full-year optimum is 3.1% above the estimate (133.25 ÷ 129.28 − 1). 0.970 is the ratio before subtracting 1, and −$3.97M is the gap in dollars (129.28 − 133.25).

In the real world

Open planning models offer the same choices. GenX's standard storage balance makes each representative period end where it starts; its long-duration storage option links the periods through the calendar, as in this lesson.
