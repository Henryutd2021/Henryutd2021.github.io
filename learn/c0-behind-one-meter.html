Behind one meter: what should one home or one plant install?

Build a plan, test what could change it, and defend it in a one-page note

Capstone C0 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: choose what the Prairie Town home or the ridge wind farm should install, run every hour of 2025 on the Site Bench’s engine, and defend the plan with its NPV, its CO₂, a sensitivity chart and what the site cannot do alone, in a one-page note.

The brief

What should one site install behind its meter: the Prairie Town home, or the ridge wind farm? You advise its owner.

  1. Start from: the owner's goal, in one line: the most money over the plan's life, or less CO₂ at a cost you state. Either can pass, with its trade-off in numbers.
  2. Hand in: a plan link and a one-page note listing every bar of the sensitivity chart in numbers; the chart stays on the page the link opens (Modeler version: one M4 comparison too).
  3. From: the Site Bench's engine, below, with every hour of 2025: solar, a battery and a heat pump, or an electrolyzer and a hydrogen tank.
  4. Done when: every check under the note is in place, and your note meets each "Look for" line of the self-check.

About 60 minutes: 25 to build and test the plan, 25 to write the note, 10 to check it.

Write your one-page note

Before you write: which number defends the plan?

On the wind farm, the bench shows two numbers that can disagree: the cost per kg of hydrogen, and what the extra equipment adds to the NPV.

As the hydrogen price rose, the optimizer built a larger electrolyzer to use more surplus wind.

The larger unit ran at lower utilization, so each kilogram carried more capital and the levelized cost of hydrogen rose, while hydrogen revenue lowered total system cost.

From: Li et al. (2024), Journal of Mechanical Design.

Among the cases studied, concentrated solar power with thermal storage running a high-temperature electrolyzer at variable power gave the lowest levelized cost of hydrogen; constant-power operation was the most expensive.

From: Li et al. (2023), EEM 2023.

Yardstick here: the cost per kilogram across designs. Holds for: one West Texas site, cost projections from NREL's Annual Technology Baseline, a conference-length study.

Say which yardstick your note uses. Case file CF2 takes the first case apart.

  1. Title and plan link: the plan's link from the bench, and the date
  2. Recommendation: Two sentences: the owner's goal, what this site should install or not, and the number that decides it.
  3. The plan: each choice, with its unit
  4. Results: each result, with its unit and boundary
  5. Assumptions: one sentence each
  6. Sensitivity: every bar of the sensitivity chart in numbers, and what was held fixed
  7. What the site cannot do alone: One or two sentences: what it still needs from the grid or a buyer, and when.
  8. What would change the answer: One sentence from your sensitivity chart: does any tested input change the recommendation, and between which values?
  9. Limits of this analysis: what the model leaves out

Check your note

Weak note · misses the Look for lines

Recommendation: Install 6 kW of solar. It saves $800 a year. [1] CO₂: Solar is clean, so the home goes green. [2] Alone: With a battery it could leave the grid. [3]

  1. Falls short: A saving without its cost: the panels cost $28,430 up front, and the NPV over 25 years is −$17,813.
  2. Falls short: No number and no boundary: bought power and gas fall from 5.9 t to 4.6 t a year (3.1 t net of the power it exports), at one average grid factor.
  3. Falls short: A claim the bench never tested: the home still buys from the grid in 5,240 hours a year.

Strong note · the three parts you write; with the bench's parts, every Look for line is met

Recommendation: The owner wants panels that pay for themselves, so do not buy 6 kW of solar at the sourced costs: NPV −$17,813 over 25 years at 4% real [1], though it saves $800 a year. CO₂: Bought power and gas fall from 5.9 t to 4.6 t a year (Kansas's average grid factor); 3.1 t net of the power it exports. [2] Alone: The home still needs the grid in 5,240 hours a year, every night. [3] Sensitivity: No tested input turns the NPV positive: −$10,237 at 75% of the equipment cost, −$14,717 with an 8¢ export credit. [4]

  1. Works: The owner's goal first, then the decision and the number that decides it, with its horizon and rate.
  2. Works: CO₂ with its size and its boundary, and the credit for exported power kept apart.
  3. Works: What the site cannot do alone, in the bench's own numbers.
  4. Works: Whether the tested ranges change the answer, from the chart's bars: here they do not, so no switching point is claimed.

C0 · The Prairie Town home: 6 kW of rooftop solar

Plan link: https://henryutd2021.github.io/learn/c0-behind-one-meter.html#c0-v1-s0-pv6-hb0-hp0-t0-x4-w150-l100-fb0-e0-k0-o1000-h4-p0-r4-c100
Made with the Site Bench (engine E0, a linear program) on 2026-11-08. Results: every hour of 2025, as 52 weeks and 31 December.

RECOMMENDATION
The owner wants panels that pay for themselves, so do not buy 6 kW of solar at the sourced costs: NPV −$17,813 over 25 years at 4% real, though it saves $800 a year. Bought power and gas fall from 5.9 t to 4.6 t a year (Kansas's average grid factor); 3.1 t net of the power it exports.

THE PLAN
- Rooftop solar: 6 kW
- Home battery: 0 kWh
- Heat pump: none (keep the gas furnace)
- Tariff: flat price
- Export credit: 4.0¢/kWh
- Discount rate (real): 4.0%
- Equipment cost: 100% of the sourced costs

RESULTS
- Net present value, 25 years: −$17,813 (against the same home with nothing new, at 4.0% real)
- Saved on bills and gas, a year: $800 ($2,114 a year before, $1,314 with the plan)
- Equipment, up front: $28,430 (upkeep $120 a year)
- CO₂ a year, bought power and gas: 4.6 t (down 1.3 t from 5.9 t; net of power sent back, credited at the same factor: 3.1 t)
- Own solar covers: 48% (of the home's electricity use, at once or through the battery)
- Hours that need the grid: 5,240 h (of 8,760; the busiest hour draws 1.5 kWh)

ASSUMPTIONS
- All money is in 2025 dollars at a 4.0% real discount rate, over 25 years.
- The weather and prices of 2025 repeat every year, and costs stay the same.
- The household use and the heating need are illustrative; the solar follows SPP's 2025 hourly pattern, scaled month by month to EIA's capacity factors and a 17.6% year.
- CO₂ counts operating emissions only: bought power at Kansas's 2024 average factor, and gas burned; power sent back is a separate credit.
- Equipment costs are 100% of the sourced values, with no tax credits or incentives.

SENSITIVITY
- Equipment cost: −$10,237 at 75% of sourced; −$25,389 at 125% of sourced
- Export credit: −$20,909 at 0.0¢/kWh; −$14,717 at 8.0¢/kWh
- Discount rate (real): −$15,162 at 2.0%; −$20,510 at 7.0%

NPV −$17,813 at this plan; each row moves one input, the equipment held fixed; re-run hour by hour: export credit.

WHAT THE SITE CANNOT DO ALONE
The home still needs the grid in 5,240 hours a year, every night.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE THE ANSWER
No tested input turns the NPV positive: −$10,237 at 75% of the equipment cost, −$14,717 with an 8¢ export credit.

LIMITS OF THIS ANALYSIS
- Storage is half full at each week's start and end, so nothing is stored from one week to the next.
- The grid is always there: outages and backup power are outside this bench.
- One average CO₂ factor for every hour: timing does not change the CO₂ here (case file CF6).

From a teaching model with stated, illustrative assumptions: not advice for a real site.

Also names who pays and who gains: the owner, the grid's other customers or the hydrogen buyer.
Uncertainty
Look for: Every bar of the sensitivity chart in the note, the equipment held fixed, and whether any tested input changes the answer.		 Single numbers, or a sensitivity without its cases. Every bar of the sensitivity chart in numbers, the equipment held fixed, and whether any tested input changes the answer. Also names the input that comes closest to changing the answer, and how far it would have to move.
Realism
Look for: What the site still needs from outside (the grid, a buyer) and what the bench leaves out (outages, storage across weeks).		 Assumes the site can do without the grid or a buyer. What the site still needs from outside, and when, and what the bench leaves out (outages, storage across weeks). Also checks one key input against its source, or a second source.
Communication
Look for: A page an owner can act on: the recommendation first, then the numbers that back it.		 Numbers without a recommendation, or a recommendation without its numbers. A one-page note with the plan link: the recommendation first, then its NPV, its CO₂, the sensitivity chart's bars in numbers and what the site cannot do alone.
