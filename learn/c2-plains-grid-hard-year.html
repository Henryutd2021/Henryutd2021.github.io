A 300 MW data center asks to connect at Prairie Town: choose what connects and what to build, test a peak-demand week and a low-wind winter week, check adequacy and show who pays

The brief

A 300 MW data center asks to connect at Prairie Town. Recommend how much of it connects this year and what the Plains Grid should build so it stays adequate through a hard year, and say who pays.

  1. Hand in: a plan link and a two-page study note with its sensitivity chart and a paragraph from M2's notebook (below the note).
  2. From: the Grid Room's engine on the fictional Plains Grid, with 2025's hourly shapes, a peak-demand week and a low-wind winter week.
  3. Done when: every check under the note is in place (the brief's 0.1 loss-of-load days a year, the chart and the notebook paragraph among them), and the self-check reaches Proficient on every competence.

About 180 minutes: 70 to build and test plans, 70 to write the note, 40 to check it.

Plus M2's notebook, about 45 minutes: run every cell. Its practice section is optional, about 90 minutes more.

In the real world

A late grid connection costs the developer lost margin and carrying costs; ratepayers face utility carrying and stranding costs; society bears external costs.

The study keeps these present-value accounts separate.

Staged energization can recover a large share of that cost.

Ratepayer exposure depends strongly on large-load tariff design.

Approved excerpt of: Li et al. (2026), accepted paper, 2026 North American Power Symposium (NAPS); DOI pending.

The full case: case file CF7. This capstone asks the network and adequacy side of the same question.

Write your two-page study note

  1. Title and plan link: the plan's link from the bench, and the date
  2. Recommendation: Three or four sentences: how much connects, what to build, and why.
  3. The plan: each choice, with its unit
  4. Results: each result, with its unit and boundary
  5. Assumptions: one sentence each
  6. Sensitivity: how one result moves when one input changes, and whether the plan was held fixed
  7. Who pays, and is it fair?: Two or three sentences: the large load, other customers, and the rule you chose.
  8. What could still go wrong: Two or three sentences: what the model leaves out that could change your answer.
  9. Three zones against one bus (M2's notebook): One paragraph, after running M2's notebook: what three zones and two links change against its one bus (prices that part, uplift, who is short).
  10. Limits of this analysis: what the model leaves out

Check your note

Weak note · Developing

Connect the data center and build some peakers [1]. The grid will be reliable [2] and prices will stay low [3], so nobody is hurt [4].

  1. Falls short: No sizes, no place, no units: the planners cannot check it.
  2. Falls short: Reliable by what measure, against what criterion, with what uncertainty?
  3. Falls short: Which prices, where, in which hours? Prices here are conditional on the commitment.
  4. Falls short: Who pays for the new plants and the link is the brief's question: name the parties and the amounts.

Strong note · Proficient

Recommendation: connect the full 300 MW this year with 6 new 50 MW peakers at Prairie Town and 300 MW more on Town–Metro [1]. Adequacy: 0.08 loss-of-load days a year (90% interval 0.05 to 0.1003: the point estimate meets the 0.1 criterion, the interval crosses it), against 0.08 for today's grid and 7.6 if the load connects with nothing built [2]. Sensitivity (chart attached): with these builds held fixed, 0.01 days at 0 MW, 0.02 days at 100 MW, 0.04 days at 200 MW, 0.08 days at 300 MW connected [3]. Hard weeks: 0 MWh unserved in the 14 days; Town's price peaks at $45/MWh, conditional on the commitment [4]. Who pays: on the 26-day estimate, the large load $91.5M a year, half the new costs directly, and other customers $72.3M a year more than today ($68.6M to $80.6M across the cost ranges); every day of 2025 gives $93.0M and $89.9M [5]. Emissions: 6,115 kt of CO₂ a year from the fuel burned [6]. Limits: two weather years, a transport model without power-flow physics, illustrative plants and costs; one study year, so it does not test a load that arrives late or never [7].

  1. Works: Sizes with units and places: a plan the planners can check and cost.
  2. Works: The criterion's measure, its Monte Carlo interval with the warning it carries, and two baselines.
  3. Works: One input moved, the plan held fixed: where the margin runs out.
  4. Works: The extreme periods kept, and the prices labelled for what they are.
  5. Works: Both parties on one boundary, a year, 2025 dollars, with the range the costs allow and the estimate checked against a full year.
  6. Works: Operating CO₂ on its own line, not mixed into the money.
  7. Works: What the study leaves out, so the planners do not over-read it.
