What you just did, as a flowchart and a checklist

Time stamps
EIA-930 stamps each hour in UTC at its end; label hours by their start and convert to local time before grouping by day.
Range, repeats, physics
Three checks flag three kinds of suspect data: a spike, a possibly stuck feed, and sun at night.
Mean or sum
Average power uses the mean; energy uses the sum, since each hour's MW × 1 h is its MWh.
Duration curve
The year's hours sorted from highest to lowest; for a plant, the area under it over the full box is the capacity factor.
Assert
A check that stops the notebook with a message when the data break a rule.
Go deeper · ModelerWhy one hour of offset matters to a model
  • An hourly model reads value t as the average power over hour t, the hour its time stamp labels; times one hour, that is its energy.
  • Shift the stamps by one hour and the solar peak moves an hour, which changes when a battery should charge and what it earns.
  • Days of 23 and 25 hours break code that assumes 24 rows a day: keep a time-zone-aware index and let pandas place each hour.

Modeler's note: the lab's notebook is the template for every later lab; upcoming M10 (forecasting) and M14 (learning from data) start from a frame checked like this one.

Go deeper · AnalystA capacity factor needs a capacity

A capacity factor is energy divided by (nameplate capacity × hours). EIA-930 reports generation, not capacity, so a real fleet's capacity factor needs a second source, such as EIA's generator inventory (Form EIA-860).

Observed peak output is not a capacity measurement. If it is below nameplate capacity, using it as the denominator overstates the capacity factor.

Prairie Town's farms are defined to reach full output in SPP's best hour, which is why their 52.8% and 23.4% are labelled illustrative. Lesson 5 builds capacity factors from scratch.

Go deeperHow the data build filled the gaps, and its night rule
Problem What the data build did
Repeated wind or solar readings Replaced by the mean of the same hour on up to seven valid days before and after.
Implausible demand hours Replaced by the day-ahead forecast, rescaled to the actual demand around the gap.
Solar at night, 20 February to 14 March Raw solar barely changed between day and night but never repeated exactly, so the repeat rule kept it. A night rule sets solar to zero.
  • The night rule is a documented heuristic for this SPP, Central-time example: hours whose monthly median solar is zero, 22:00 to 05:00 all year, and hours when the sun is below the horizon at SPP's centre.
  • What it proves. Hours set to zero pass a night check by construction; the check does not test the daytime values, so look at those too.
  • The notebook applies the median and clock parts of the rule to the raw file.

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “Every local day has 24 hours.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Where clocks change for daylight saving, one spring day has 23 hours and one autumn day has 25; in 2025 in U.S. Central time, 9 March and 2 November.

Myth or fact? “If the summary statistics look normal, every row is fine.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. 617 MW is a normal solar value, but not at 2 a.m.; repeat and physics checks look at rows and their times, not just at summaries.

Myth or fact? “Average wind output divided by the year's best hour is the wind fleet's capacity factor.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. A capacity factor divides by nameplate capacity. The best hour is not a capacity measurement; if it is below nameplate, that ratio overstates the capacity factor.

A wind farm's hourly output is 100 MW in every hour of one 24-hour day. How much energy did it make that day?

  • 100 MWh
  • 2,400 MWh
  • 4.2 MW
  • 2,400 MW
Answer

2,400 MWh: energy = power × time, 100 MW × 24 h. On a 23- or 25-hour day it would be 2,300 or 2,500 MWh.

You resample hourly MW values to months. Which gives each month's energy?

  • The mean
  • The sum
  • The maximum
  • The median
Answer

The sum, in MWh (divide by 1,000 for GWh). The mean gives the month's average power in MW.
