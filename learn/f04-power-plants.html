What you just did, as diagrams

Most plants spin a generator
Gas, steam, hydro, geothermal and wind plants all turn a turbine that turns a generator; solar panels have no moving parts.
Heat plants lose most as waste heat
EIA's 2025 full-load tests imply that U.S. combined-cycle gas plants send about 45.3 of every 100 units of fuel energy to the wire, coal steam plants 34.0; the rest leaves as waste heat.
Heat rate and efficiency are one number
Share to the wire = 3,412 Btu ÷ the heat rate in Btu per kWh: 3,412 ÷ 7,535 = 45.3%.
Wind and solar follow the weather
The 200 MW farm made 776 MWh on 5 September 2025 and 1,666 MWh on 11 September; its output alone cannot show which weather made the gap.
Stored water and ramp rates set limits
A reservoir with no inflow runs dry after so many hours at full flow, and a plant's ramp rate caps how fast it can follow demand.
Go deeper · AnalystHeat rate, efficiency and why hotter heat helps

Two views of one number. Efficiency \(\eta\) (a share, no unit) and heat rate \(HR\) (Btu of fuel per kWh sent out):

\[ \eta = \frac{3{,}412\ \text{Btu per kWh}}{HR}. \]

EIA’s heat rates are net: they divide the fuel by the electricity the plant sends out. EIA typically counts fuel at its gross heat content, which includes the heat in the exhaust’s water vapour (EIA glossary).

Hotter heat helps. A heat engine can turn a larger share of its heat into work when that heat is hotter than its cooling water.

Heat source How hot Of 100 units, to the wire
Geothermal water 300 to 700 °F (EIA) about 16 (EIA, typical)
Gas in a gas turbine above 2,000 °F (DOE) 20 to 35 alone (DOE)

The physics of that ceiling, and how engineers approach it, is device thermodynamics, beyond this lesson (side topic X1).

  • Combined cycle. The gas turbine’s exhaust is still hot enough to boil water for a steam turbine. With that second turbine, EIA’s 2025 full-load tests imply about 45.3% for U.S. combined-cycle plants; DOE reports efficiencies as high as 60 percent for its advanced turbines.
  • Measured against notional. The gas and coal shares come from tested heat rates at full load, not a year’s fuel use; the hydro, wind and geothermal shares are EIA’s typical equipment values. Wind, sunlight, falling water and the earth’s heat are not bought by the unit, so the energy a plant does not catch carries no significant cost (EIA, AER 2010, Appendix F).
Go deeper · ModelerPlant limits as rules in a model

A dispatch model chooses each plant’s output in every hour so that supply meets demand at the lowest cost, within each plant’s rules:

  • Size: output stays between zero and the plant’s size, in MW.
  • Ramp: from one hour to the next, output changes by no more than the ramp limit (MW per hour) times the hour.
  • Fuel: fuel burned is the heat rate times the electricity made.
  • Stored water: the electricity made over the period (MWh) stays within what the usable water can give at its head.
  • Weather: wind and solar output stay within what the wind or the sun allows that hour.

The fuel rule, with output \(p_t\) in MW held for \(\Delta t\) hours, \(HR\) in Btu per kWh and 1,000 kW in a MW:

\[ F_t = 1{,}000 \cdot HR \cdot p_t\,\Delta t \quad \text{(Btu)}. \]

At 150 MW for one hour and 7,535 Btu per kWh, that is about 1.13 billion Btu.

Lesson 0 followed the evening greedily, one hour at a time; a model that sees the whole day can warm the slow plant up early. M1 writes rules like these as a model and solves it, M2 adds start-ups and minimum output, and lesson 9 treats a reservoir like a battery.

Check yourself

Practice, about 10 minutes: four myths, then four questions.

Myth or fact? “A modern power plant turns nearly all the fuel it burns into electricity.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. EIA's 2025 full-load tests imply about 45.3% for U.S. combined-cycle gas plants and 34.0% for coal steam plants; the rest leaves as waste heat.

Myth or fact? “A plant with a low share to the wire is always wasting something it paid for.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. A solar panel sends nearly 25 of 100 units to the wire, but sunlight is not bought fuel: the energy it does not catch carries no significant cost (EIA, AER 2010, Appendix F).

Myth or fact? “A hydro dam can run at full power for as long as it likes.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It runs until the reservoir runs low: with no inflow, the lesson's 20 million m³ last 55.6 hours at 100 m³/s. A run-of-river plant has almost no store at all.

Myth or fact? “The fastest gas plant is also the one that gets the most electricity from its gas.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. At full load, a peaker (a gas turbine alone) sends about 31.1% of its fuel energy to the wire and a combined cycle about 45.3% (EIA's 2025 tests). Speed and efficiency are different jobs.

A gas peaker has a heat rate of 10,975 Btu per kWh. What share of its fuel energy reaches the wire?

  • 31.1%
  • 68.9%
  • 45.3%
  • 321.7%
Answer

31.1%: 3,412 ÷ 10,975 = 0.311. The other 68.9% leaves as waste heat in the hot exhaust; dividing the other way round gives an impossible 321.7%.

A hydro plant doubles its head and keeps the same flow. What happens to its power?

  • It stays the same
  • It doubles
  • It grows eight times
  • It halves
Answer

It doubles: power = density × gravity × flow × head × efficiency, so it grows in step with the head. The cube law belongs to wind (lesson 5).

On 5 September 2025 the 200 MW solar farm made 776 MWh. What was its capacity factor for that day?

  • 388%
  • 32.3%
  • 16.2%
  • 47%
Answer

16.2%: 776 ÷ (200 × 24). Counting only 12 daylight hours would give 32.3%, but the night counts too; the high-output 11 September reached 34.7%.

Which kind of plant usually goes from cold to full output fastest?

  • A coal steam plant
  • A gas combined-cycle plant
  • A geothermal plant
  • A hydro plant
Answer

Hydro: most hydro turbines reach full output in under 10 minutes; most combined-cycle plants take 1 to 12 hours and coal plants more than half a day (EIA, 2020).

In the real world

  1. Turbines everywhere: in 2022, steam turbines made about 42% of U.S. electricity, combined-cycle plants about 34%, wind turbines about 10%, hydro turbines about 6% and solar panels about 3% (EIA).
  2. Panels keep improving: commercial solar panels averaged less than 10% in the mid-1980s; today's best modules approach 25% (EIA).
  3. A turbine's output is not one number: it depends on the wind speed, through its power curve (lesson 5).

From the course author's research: a wind turbine's real power curve is a band, not a line. Adding the turbine's theoretical curve to about two hundred measurements can narrow that band, but whether it helps depends on how the fit is scored. (Li et al., ASME IDETC 2023)
