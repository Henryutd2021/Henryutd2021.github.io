Model a city’s energy system: an energy hub with grid limits and screening fairness outputs

Write Prairie City’s energy hub as a linear program with a ceiling on each substation, read its duals, let it choose under a CO2 cap, and keep the household estimates apart

Modeler lesson M12 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: a fictional city’s heat pumps, retrofits, district heat, car charging, rooftop solar and batteries in one linear program with a limit on each district’s substation; the limits’ and a CO2 cap’s duals; bills by household group as screening estimates from an accounting layer.

With JavaScript on, you run each plan with a real solver in your browser, read the substations' duals, let the LP choose under a CO₂ cap, and change who adopts first in the household table. Prairie City is fictional; its numbers are illustrative.

  • Illustrative: Prairie City, its three districts, one substation each and its household groups (prairie-city.json); retrofit, district-heat and upgrade costs are set for teaching, fixed here at their defaults (the inputs table lists them; the City Planner changes them).
  • Based on public data: Kansas's 2025 prices (EIA), Wichita's weather normals (NOAA), the Southwest Power Pool's (SPP's) 2025 hourly shapes (EIA-930), eGRID's 2023 SPP North CO₂ rate (EPA), DOE LEAD's income bands.
  • Costs: heat pumps and rooftop solar from EIA, batteries from NREL, 2025 dollars.
  • Time: eight representative days of 2025, weighted to 365 days; a cold design day and the summer peak day kept.
  • Weather: NOAA's normals on SPP's 2025 days (synthetic). The full-year check, below the steps, runs the four plans on every hour of 2025.
  • Solver: HiGHS (open source), loaded on your first run (about 3.5 MB, once).

Build it in Python · notebook lab, about 60 minutes, then 120 minutes of practice

The engine's hub with scipy.optimize.linprog and HiGHS.

  • The LP: balances, substation rows and their duals, managed charging, batteries, the heat network.
  • Choosing: the building shares as decisions under a CO₂ cap.
  • After the LP: the household layer.

What it checks: assert cells match the browser's busiest hours, upgrades, costs, CO₂, the duals and the LP's chosen shares.

  1. Download the notebook and the two data files; put them in one folder.
  2. Upload all three to Google Colab (File → Upload notebook, then the folder icon), or open the notebook locally with Python 3, NumPy and SciPy 1.9 or later.
  3. Run all cells, then try the practice tasks at the end.

Go deeper · ModelerThe hub in symbols

Every hour lasts \(\Delta t = 1\) h, so a MW held for an hour is a MWh. Days \(k\) (8), hours \(h\) (24), districts \(d\) (3).

Symbol What it is Unit Decided or given
\(imp_{dkh}\) bought through district \(d\)’s substation MW decided, \(\ge 0\)
\(pv_{dkh}\), \(ev_{dkh}\) rooftop solar used; car charging (managed) MW decided
\(c_{dkh}\), \(o_{dkh}\), \(q_{dkh}\) battery in, out, stored MW, MW, MWh decided
\(y_{d,e,s}\) share of district \(d\)’s heat in envelope \(e\) (0 as built, 1 retrofitted) with system \(s\) share given in a run; decided in step 6
\(up_d\) upgrade of \(d\)’s substation MW decided
\(w_k\) days day \(k\) stands for (they add to 365) days given
\(p_d\), \(p_{ind}\), \(a\) \(d\)’s 2025 electricity price; the industrial price the network’s plant pays; the upgrade’s yearly cost $/MWh; $/MWh; $ per MW a year given
\(B_{dkh}\), \(Q_{dkh}\) base load; heat needed: the hour’s temperature deficit below 18.3 °C × the heat-loss rate (MW of heat per °C) MW; MW of heat given (prairie-city.json)
\(\sigma\), \(COP_{kh}\) heat a retrofit saves; the heat pump’s coefficient of performance (heat out per unit of electricity in) share; MWh/MWh given
\(L_d\), \(S_{dkh}\), \(E_{dk}\) substation limit; rooftop output; the day’s charging energy MW; MW; MWh given
\(pl_{kh}\) the heat network’s plant power (core only): its heat pump’s heat \(\div\) 3 MW decided
\(hp_{kh}\), \(boil_{kh}\), \(si_{kh}\), \(so_{kh}\) the network’s heat pump, gas boiler (assumed to exist: fuel only), store in and out MW of heat decided

\[\min\; \sum_k w_k \sum_h \Big[\sum_d p_d\, imp_{dkh} + (p_{ind} - p_{core})\, pl_{kh}\Big] \;+\; a \sum_d up_d \;+\; \text{fuel}(y, boil) \;[+\; \text{equipment}(y, \text{sizes})]\]

The plant’s power comes through the core’s substation but is bought at the industrial price: the second term swaps one price for the other. Fuel is the furnaces’ gas and propane (set by \(y\)) and the network boiler’s gas: \(\sum_k w_k \sum_h boil_{kh} \div 0.9\) at the commercial gas price.

Each district-hour (the balance). Its dual carries the day’s weight \(w_k\): divided by \(w_k\), it is the marginal cost of one more MWh in one such hour, as the engine reports it. The substation duals stay summed over the year, in $ per MW a year.

\[imp + pv + o - c - ev - \sum_{e,s} Q_{dkh}\,(1 - \sigma e)\; y_{d,e,s}\;\kappa_s - [d = \text{core}]\, pl_{kh} = B_{dkh}, \qquad \kappa_{res} = 1,\; \kappa_{hp} = \frac{1}{COP_{kh}},\; \kappa_{fuel} = \kappa_{dh} = 0\]

The heat network in the core (its own heat balance, with 10% lost in the pipes and a store that loses 0.5% an hour):

\[hp_{kh} + boil_{kh} + so_{kh} - si_{kh} = \frac{1}{0.9}\sum_{e} Q_{\text{core},kh}\,(1 - \sigma e)\; y_{\text{core},e,dh}, \qquad pl_{kh} = \frac{hp_{kh}}{3}\]

The substation (its dual: what one more MW of room is worth that hour), the sun, the battery and the day’s charging:

\[imp_{dkh} - up_d \le L_d, \qquad pv_{dkh} \le S_{dkh}, \qquad q_{dkh} = q_{dk,h-1} + 0.85\, c_{dkh} - o_{dkh},\; q_{dk,-1} = q_{dk,23}, \qquad \sum_h ev_{dkh} = E_{dk}\]

When the LP chooses (step 6): \(\sum_{e,s} y_{d,e,s} = 1\), furnaces and resistance no more than in 2025, retrofits and district heat up to their maxima, and \(\text{CO}_2(imp, y) \le cap\). Shares sit inside the LP in percent (0 to 100), which keeps HiGHS’s numbers close in size.

Go deeper · ModelerWhen two answers cost the same: the tie rule

With one price all day, many schedules tie. Small stated costs pick one; they are left out of every reported cost total, though the duals include them:

Tie cost Size
Each district's busiest hour $20 a year per MW
The three districts' busiest hour together $10 per MW
Out of a battery $1 per MWh
Out of the heat store $0.50 per MWh
Managed charging $0.10 per MWh × the hour's share of SPP's peak demand
  • The LP minimizes the cost plus these small costs: on a few plans it pays under $30 a year, in the engine's test, to keep a busiest hour that is under its limit lower.
  • The engine's test finds the lowest and highest value of each busiest hour, each upgrade and the energy bought over every plan within $1 of the best cost: they agree within its tolerances (0.5 MW, 0.05 MW and 5 MWh).
  • The tie costs also enter the balance duals, by a few dollars at most on the busiest hours: read them as "about".
  • The substation duals summed over the year are checked as slopes of the LP's objective, cost plus the tie costs: it moves by the same amount when the limit moves 0.1 MW up or down.
  • The reported cost leaves the tie costs out: where a limit binds without an upgrade, it saves about $9 per MW a year more than the dual.
Go deeper · ResearcherWhere this model stops
  • One ceiling per substation stands for a whole distribution network; real planning checks each feeder's power flow and voltage (A17).
  • Each day ends with the stored energy it started with, a level the LP picks day by day, on the eight days and in the full-year check: no heat or charge carried between days.
  • The full-year check uses the same synthetic weather rule: it tests the choice of days, not another weather year.
  • The base load follows the whole regional grid's 2025 shape, which keeps some of its winter electric heating: winter peaks and upgrades are illustrative.
  • Many plans cost nearly the same; methods that map near-optimal plans show the choice is wider than one optimum.

Check yourself

Electrifying heat only adds yearly kWh; the grid sees a small rise.

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Cold weather, when heat pumps' COP is low, lifts heating in every home at once; with the base load and car charging it sets the busiest hour (06:00 or 20:00 here), which the yearly total hides.

Where the LP buys an upgrade, the substation's duals summed over the year equal the upgrade's yearly cost per MW.

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. One more MW of limit saves one MW of upgrade; the test checks it as a cost slope both ways.

The LP tells you how a plan changes each household group's energy burden.

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. The LP gives the city's cost, CO₂ and peaks; who pays comes from an accounting layer with its own stated assumptions.
