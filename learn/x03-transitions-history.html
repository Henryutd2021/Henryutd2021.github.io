  • Town and other non-farm homes passed half between 1920 and 1925; farm homes between 1945 and 1950, about 25 years later.
  • Coal heated 54.7% of homes in 1940 and 2.9% in 1970; gas rose from 11.3% (with bottled gas) to 55.2%. Gas overtook coal as the most common main heating fuel between the 1950 and 1960 censuses.
  • Other fuels, such as heating oil, changed too; these census snapshots do not track homes switching from one fuel to another.
  • Families owning a car: 54% in 1948, 82% in 1970. Registered cars rose from 0.46 million in 1910 to 23.0 million in 1930.
  • Solar made 1.0% of U.S. electricity in 2015 and 8.7% in 2025, counting rooftop panels (our arithmetic on EIA's table).

What you just did, as diagrams

Decades, not years
On its diffusion curve, electric service went from 8% of U.S. homes in 1907 to over 90% between 1945 and 1950.
Not everywhere at once
Farms passed half about 25 years after town homes; wires had to reach them first.
Gas overtook coal
Piped gas overtook coal as the most common main heating fuel between the 1950 and 1960 censuses. Other fuels changed too; the snapshots do not track homes switching.
What sped it
Networks built, public programmes and a cheaper or better service sped curves up; refining a new source until it could compete took decades.
Say what you measure
A device in homes, a fuel's share of a nation and a share of electricity move at different speeds.

U.S. energy transitions, dated

  1. 1850 · Coal at 9% of U.S. energy. Coal supplied 9% of U.S. energy use, and 27% by 1870 as railroads spread. Source: EIA, Today in Energy, 30 June 2026
  2. 1882 · Pearl Street Station. One of the world's first central power plants opened in New York; it could power 5,000 lights. Source: EIA, Energy Kids, electricity timeline
  3. 1885 · Coal passes wood. Around 1885 coal passed wood as the largest U.S. energy source. Source: EIA, Today in Energy, 30 June 2026
  4. 1907 · 8% of homes have electric service. The first year of the Census Bureau's series of homes with electric service. Source: Census Bureau, Historical Statistics (1975), Series S 109-111
  5. 1908 · Model T. Ford first produced the Model T car. Source: EIA, Energy Kids, transportation timeline
  6. 1936 · Rural Electrification Act. A law aimed at bringing electricity to farms across the country. Source: EIA, Energy Kids, electricity timeline
  7. 1950 · Petroleum passes coal. Petroleum became the most-used U.S. energy source, and has been ever since. Source: EIA, Today in Energy, 30 June 2026
  8. 1956 · Interstate Highway System. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 established the Interstate Highway System. Source: EIA, Energy Kids, transportation timeline
  9. 1958 · Natural gas passes coal. Natural gas passed coal in U.S. energy use; it has been second most years since. Source: EIA, Today in Energy, 2 July 2025
  10. 1970 · Gas heats most homes. Piped gas heated 55.2% of homes and coal or coke 2.9%, against 11.3% (with bottled gas) and 54.7% in 1940. Source: Census Bureau, House Heating Fuel, 1940-2000
  11. 2023 · Renewables pass coal. U.S. energy use from renewables passed coal for the first time since the 1880s. Source: EIA, Today in Energy, 30 June 2026
  12. 2025 · Solar at 8.7% of electricity. Solar made 8.7% of U.S. electricity, utility-scale and small-scale together (our arithmetic). Source: EIA, Electric Power Annual, Table 3.1.A

Where we stop

This side topic stops at reading the curves

Adoption enters the course only as diffusion, a share over time; costs and forecasts stay out.

This side topic covers

  • What share of homes, families or electricity each change reached, and when.
  • What sped some curves up and held others back, read from dated sources.
  • Why the answer to 'how fast' depends on what you measure.

Left to specialists

  • How the cost of any technology changed over time.
  • Forecasts of how fast anything spreads next.
  • Economic history beyond these curves: prices, wages, trade.

  2. Go further. How long national and global transitions took, country by country. Vaclav Smil, Energy Transitions: Global and National Perspectives (Praeger), cited by name, second edition, 2017

Go deeper · AnalystReading a diffusion curve without fooling yourself
  • Say the whole. A share of homes, of families and of electricity are three different wholes; a curve means nothing until its denominator is named.
  • Published points only. The page reports a year as “between 1945 and 1950” rather than drawing a smooth line through gaps.
  • Starts and ends. The car survey began when most families already had a car; solar’s curve stops at the latest year of data.
  • Services first. Grubler (2012, abstract) points to energy end use and services, and to the lengthy process of transitions.
Go deeperWhat this lesson leaves out
  • Countries other than the United States, and the world's energy mix before 1900.
  • Prices, wages and trade: the economic history behind each curve.
  • How the cost of any technology changed over time.
  • Any forecast of how fast a curve will move next.

Check yourself

About a minute: two myths.

Myth or fact? “Farm homes and town homes passed half with electric service at about the same time.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Town and other non-farm homes passed half between 1920 and 1925; farm homes only between 1945 and 1950, about 25 years later (Census Bureau).

Myth or fact? “Petroleum has been the largest source of U.S. energy since 1950.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. Petroleum passed coal in 1950; coal had passed wood around 1885 (EIA, 2026).
