Deciding before the uncertainty resolves: stochastic and robust optimization

Size an electrolyzer before anyone knows how much wind gets built: plan for the average, hedge across the branches, or guard the worst case

  • Expected value of perfect information: $671.10M − $653.54M = $17.56M a year.

With JavaScript on, you solve each plan yourself, step by step, with a solver in your browser.

  • Toy: a fictional windy zone with 3,000 MW of wind and 4,000 MW planned; its lines take 1,000 MW. Chances, costs and demand are illustrative round numbers in 2025 dollars.
  • Wind blocks: the 8,760 hours of 2025 sorted by output and cut into five blocks: SPP's hourly wind shape (EIA-930) scaled to 33.8%, U.S. wind's 2025 capacity factor (EIA, Table 6.07.B).
  • Electrolyzer: it uses electricity to make hydrogen from water. $80,000 per MW a year; 18 kg of hydrogen per MWh; it runs only on surplus wind.
  • Hydrogen: industry takes 54,000 kg an hour; gas reformers, which make hydrogen from natural gas and steam, supply the rest at $1.50 per kg.

About 45 guided minutes in eleven steps. Solver: HiGHS (open source), loaded on your first solve (about 3.5 MB, once).

Go deeper · ModelerThe extensive form in algebra, and the order of the costs

For branches \(s \in \{\text{none}, \text{half}, \text{all}\}\) and wind blocks \(b = 1 \dots 5\), one linear program holds the size and every branch’s operation (the extensive form, also called the deterministic equivalent):

\[\min_{x,\,e,\,r}\; c\,x + \sum_s p_s \sum_b h_b\, c_r\, r_{s,b}\]

\[ \begin{aligned} k\,e_{s,b} + r_{s,b} &= H && \text{(hydrogen, every branch and block)}\\ e_{s,b} &\le x && \text{(the size links the stages)}\\ 0 \le e_{s,b} &\le \sigma_{s,b} = \max(0,\; W_s f_b - A), \quad r_{s,b} \ge 0,\; x \ge 0 \end{aligned} \]

Symbol Meaning Value
\(x\) electrolyzer size, chosen here and now MW
\(c\) yearly cost of a MW of electrolyzer $80,000
\(p_s\) chance of branch \(s\) 0.25, 0.5, 0.25
\(h_b\), \(f_b\) hours a year and wind capacity factor of block \(b\) 500 to 3,760 h; 0.571 to 0.194
\(e_{s,b}\), \(r_{s,b}\) electrolyzer power (MW) and reformer hydrogen (kg an hour): recourse
\(k\), \(H\), \(c_r\) 18 kg per MWh; 54,000 kg an hour; $1.50 per kg
\(W_s\), \(A\) wind built in branch \(s\); what the lines take 3,000, 5,000, 7,000 MW; 1,000 MW
  • Size. 31 columns and 30 rows; with \(n\) branches it has \(1 + 10n\) columns, so it grows with every branch you add.
  • The order of the costs. WS ≤ RP ≤ EEV: here $653.54M ≤ $671.10M ≤ $673.72M. So EVPI = RP − WS and VSS = EEV − RP are both zero or more (Birge and Louveaux, ch. 4).
  • Robust plans. Add a free variable \(z\) and minimise it, with \(z \ge \text{cost}_s(x)\) in every branch (worst case), or \(z \ge \text{cost}_s(x) - \text{WS}_s\) (least regret). Neither row uses \(p_s\).
Go deeper · ModelerWhy 845 MW: the critical-hours rule
  • One more MW. It costs $80,000 a year and saves $1.50 × 18 = $27 for every hour it runs on surplus. It pays while it runs more than $80,000 ÷ $27 = 2,963 hours a year.
  • In each branch. Just below its own best size, 380 MW (none built), 1,300 MW (half) or 2,220 MW (all), an extra MW runs 3,000 hours; just above, 1,500. Each wait-and-see plan sits where its count falls below 2,963.
  • Over the branches. Weighted by the chances, an extra MW just below 845 MW runs 3,750 hours; just above, 2,750. The stochastic plan sits where the expected count crosses 2,963.
  • When the rule stops. With one size, no storage and one zone, it finds the plan by hand. A tank, several zones or pipelines couple the blocks and sizes; that needs a coupled model, here a linear program.
Go deeperWhat this toy leaves out
  • Risk: the stochastic plan weighs only expected cost. A planner who fears the bad branches can add a risk measure, such as the expected cost of the worst 10% of outcomes.
  • Later decisions: here the size is chosen once. A multi-stage program lets the planner build more after part of the uncertainty resolves.
  • Where the branches come from: three hand-picked branches with judged chances. More branches drawn by sampling bring sampling error, as in M7.
  • Time: five blocks of a duration curve, so no storage and no hour-to-hour links. Representative periods with storage are M3's subject.
  • Space: one zone, no pipelines, steady hydrogen demand and a fixed price for reformer hydrogen.

Check yourself, then try it

Myth or fact? “Planning for the average future gives you the average cost.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. Here the plan for 5,000 MW of wind expects $662.63M a year; priced in the three branches with their chances, it costs $673.72M.

Myth or fact? “A robust plan is the best plan by every yardstick.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. The worst-case plan has the lowest worst cost, $703.40M, but it costs $12.16M a year more in expectation than the stochastic plan.

Myth or fact? “The expected value of perfect information is what a real forecast of the build-out is worth.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It is the most a perfect forecast is worth, under these chances, to a planner who weighs only expected cost. A forecast that can be wrong is worth less.

The plan for the average costs $50M in expectation, the stochastic plan $48M, and the wait-and-see plans $45M weighted by their chances. VSS and EVPI?

  • VSS $2M, EVPI $3M
  • VSS $3M, EVPI $2M
  • VSS $5M, EVPI $3M
  • VSS $2M, EVPI $5M
Answer

VSS = $50M − $48M = $2M; EVPI = $48M − $45M = $3M.

You change the branches' chances. Which plan stays the same?

  • The stochastic plan
  • The plan for the average
  • The worst-case plan
  • All three move
Answer

The worst-case plan: it uses the set of build-outs, not their chances. Here it stays at 380 MW.

Where this came up in research

A two-stage stochastic linear program sized electrolyzers, hydrogen tanks and fuel cells once in each of ERCOT's eight weather zones, with hydrogen pipelines between neighbouring zones, then scheduled the system hour by hour over a full year in nine scenarios: three completion rates of planned renewable capacity by three electricity-demand levels.

Hydrogen equipment is sized once, then operated hour by hour in nine scenarios; the sizes minimize expected cost over all nine scenarios instead of fitting a single forecast.

The completion rate of planned renewable capacity was 0% (probability 0.1), 30% (0.7) or 80% (0.2); electricity demand was ERCOT's 2030 projection (0.5), 2% lower (0.2) or 2% higher (0.3).

From: Li et al. (2025), International Journal of Hydrogen Energy.

Inputs reflect 2022–2023 conditions; post-2023 federal hydrogen credit rules are not modeled.

This page's toy is one zone with round numbers, not the study's inputs, equations or results. The whole case: CF3, where hydrogen should go when nobody knows how much wind will be built.
