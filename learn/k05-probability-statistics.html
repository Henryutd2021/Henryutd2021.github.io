Probability and statistics

Read four months of real forecast errors as a distribution: centre, spread, tails and chance

Math toolbox K5 of Energy Systems, Step by Step: with 2,716 real hourly day-ahead forecast errors from the Southwest Power Pool (January–April 2025), build a histogram, compare mean and median, measure spread, read percentiles and intervals, estimate a chance by counting and by sampling, and combine two plants’ outages into a joint chance and an expected value.

2,716 hourly forecast errors, January–April 2025

  1. A histogram counts hours per bin: with 1-point bins, 74% of the 2,716 hours lie from −2% up to +3%; the errors run from −8.3% to +9.8%.
  2. Centre: mean +0.8%, median +0.7%. Demand came in above the forecast in 61% of hours.
  3. Spread: standard deviation 2.45 points; mean ± 1 SD, from −1.65% to +3.25%, holds 72% of hours; the quartiles are −0.8% and +2.2%.
  4. Chance by counting: 134 of 2,716 hours, 4.9%, had demand more than 5% above the forecast.
  5. Nominal intervals from percentiles: 50% from −0.8% to +2.2%; 80% from −2.1% to +3.8% (lesson 4's band); 95% from −3.5% to +6.2%. Hours tied at the ends make them hold 51.3%, 81.1% and 95.0%.
  6. Samples scatter: the standard error of that share is 6.8 points for 10 random hours, 2.2 for 100 and 0.7 for 1,000.
  7. Independence (illustrative): gas plants A and B, each out in 5% of hours, are both out in 0.25% of hours, about 22 a year, only if their outages are independent.
  8. Expected value: with plants A (150 MW) and B (100 MW) each out 5% of the time, 12.5 MW are out on average.

What you just did, as diagrams

Histogram
Hours counted per bin of error; the bin width changes how jagged it looks, not the data.
Mean and median
Two centres: the average, pulled by every value, and the middle value, which a few wild values barely move.
Standard deviation, quartiles
Two spreads: a typical distance from the mean, and the range of the middle half.
Percentile and interval
The value a given share of hours stays at or below; two of them bound an interval.
Probability as a share
Count the hours where the event happened and divide by all hours; with few hours, the share is rough.
Independence
When one event says nothing about the other, the chance of both is the product; a shared cause breaks it.
Expected value
Each outcome times its chance, added up; expected values add with or without independence.
Go deeper · AnalystThe formulas, and how sure a sample is
  • Mean. \(\bar e = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i\), where \(e_i\) is hour \(i\)’s error in % and \(n = 2716\) hours.
  • Standard deviation. \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (e_i - \bar e)^2}\), in percentage points. Dividing by \(n - 1\), as many tools do, gives 2.4533 points against 2.4528: with this many hours the choice hardly matters.
  • Percentile. Sort the errors and write the share as a fraction \(p\) (\(p = 0.9\) for the 90th percentile). The percentile sits at position \((n - 1)\,p\) counting from 0, between two neighbours when that is not a whole number.
  • How sure a share is. Draw \(m\) hours at random and find the share \(\hat p\) above +5%. It scatters by about \(\sqrt{\hat p\,(1 - \hat p)/m}\), its standard error: for \(\hat p\) = 4.9%, 6.8 points for \(m\) = 10 hours, 2.2 for 100 and 0.7 for 1,000.
  • Two standard errors. For large samples, about 95% of estimates land within two standard errors. With 10 hours the rule is rough: the share can only be 0%, 10%, 20% and so on.
  • Draws with replacement. Each draw can pick any hour, even one already drawn, so the draws are independent with the same chances. Real hours in a row may move together, which this sampling leaves out.
Go deeper · ModelerDistributions as model inputs (a preview)
  • Monte Carlo. Draw a model’s uncertain inputs from their distributions, run the model each time, and read percentiles of the result (Modeler lesson M7).
  • Scenarios with chances. A stochastic program is an optimization model that plans against several scenarios at once, weighted by their chances, to minimise expected cost: step 8’s expected value inside a model (M8).
  • Surrogates. A surrogate is a fast stand-in fitted to a slow model’s runs. One kind, a Gaussian-process model, gives each prediction a mean and a standard deviation (M9).
  • Draw together what moves together. Sample whole hours or days, so demand, wind and outages keep their joint behaviour; drawing each input separately assumes independence.

Check yourself

Myth or fact? “Next winter, 95% of hours will fall inside this page's 95% interval.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth. It holds 95% of the 2,716 hours it was built from, by construction. Other months are untested, and from May the two reported series disagree.

Myth or fact? “Two plants that are each out in 5% of hours are both out in 0.25% of hours.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Myth, as a general rule. The product holds only if the outages are independent; a shared cause makes both-out hours more common.

Myth or fact? “One wildly wrong reading moves the median less than the mean.”

  • Myth
  • Fact
Answer

Fact. Adding one +100% reading to these hours moves the mean by +0.04 points and the median by 0.

Five hourly errors: −1%, 0%, +1%, +2%, +8%. Their mean and median?

  • Mean +2%, median +1%
  • Mean +1%, median +2%
  • Both +2%
  • Mean +2%, median 0%
Answer

Mean +2% (the sum, +10%, over 5); median +1%, the middle of the sorted five. The +8% pulls the mean up, not the median.

A random sample of 100 of these hours has 9% above +5%; all 2,716 hours have 4.9%. The best reading?

  • The forecast got worse
  • A share from 100 hours is rough; 9% is within its usual scatter
  • 9% is the true chance
  • Samples cannot tell you anything
Answer

Rough: with 100 hours the standard error is about 2.2 points, and 4.9% + 2 × 2.2 ≈ 9.3%, so 9% is within two standard errors.
